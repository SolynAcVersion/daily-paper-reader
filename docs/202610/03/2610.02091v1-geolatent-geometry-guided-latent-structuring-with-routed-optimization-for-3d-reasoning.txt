ABSTRACT 

Despite progress in vision-language models, 3D spatial reasoning from 2D im-ages remains challenging. Text-based methods describe intermediate geometry with discrete tokens, limiting fidelity for continuous spatial relations. Continu-ous latents offer richer representations, but a single latent type does not explicitly separate the cues needed across spatial tasks. Decomposed spatial latents address this by representing position, direction, and global geometry separately under ge-ometric supervision. Yet the geometry representation can still collapse toward one dominant direction, and unrestricted attention can leave the latents underused during answer learning. We introduce GeoLatent , combining Common–Residual Geometry Alignment (CR-GEO) with routed optimization to structure the geom-etry states while promoting latent-mediated answer learning. CR-GEO separates shared from residual teacher geometry; routed optimization jointly trains geome-try and language, temporarily directs visual answer learning through the latents, and restores full attention with geometry supervision. In controlled comparisons, CR-GEO raises geometry effective rank from 1.00 to 3.87 , while blocking latent readout at the bottleneck lowers direction accuracy from 89 .1% to 25 .8% on 128 fixed questions. After recovery, the differentiated geometry representation and latent-mediated visual route remain available alongside direct image access. Ge-oLatent achieves 73 .0% on SPAR-Bench and 72 .1% on SPBench, outperforming previously reported methods on both. 

1 INTRODUCTION 

Vision-language models (VLMs) have achieved remarkable progress in visual understanding and reasoning, yet 3D spatial reasoning remains challenging (Zhang et al., 2025b; Li et al., 2026d). Spa-tial reasoning tasks require models to infer geometric properties such as depth, distance, relative position, orientation, and scene layout from 2D observations. To improve these capabilities, exist-ing studies have explored spatial instruction tuning, progressive training, and explicit 3D or 2.5D representations (Chen et al., 2024; Li et al., 2026d; Wu et al., 2025a). However, when intermedi-ate reasoning is explicitly modeled, it is still commonly expressed as text-based chain-of-thought. Continuous geometric information must then be represented through discrete language or numeri-cal tokens, which may lose fine-grained spatial detail and make the reasoning more susceptible to language priors (Li et al., 2026c). Continuous latent reasoning provides an alternative by representing intermediate reasoning in con-tinuous hidden states rather than expressing every step as natural-language tokens (Shen et al., 2025; Li et al., 2026e). For spatial reasoning, however, these latent representations need to capture task-relevant spatial cues at different levels of granularity: some tasks require precise object positions or relative directions, whereas others depend on more global scene geometry. With a single latent type, spatial cues with different functions and levels of granularity have to share the same form of latent representation, making it difficult to accommodate the diverse demands of spatial reasoning tasks. These latent components are trained with corresponding geometric supervision. However, it remains un-clear whether geometric supervision alone is sufficient to yield effective latent representations and whether these latent intermediates consistently contribute to subsequent reasoning and answer pre-diction. Our analysis reveals two limitations that geometric supervision alone does not resolve. First, when the teacher geometry contains a dominant shared component, GeoAnchor’s geometry alignment objective can lead multiple geometry states to learn highly similar representations rather than cap-ture complementary scene structure. We observe that the resulting geometry representation has an effective rank close to one, indicating substantial redundancy across its states. Second, inserting decomposed spatial latents into the reasoning trajectory does not ensure that subsequent reasoning and answer prediction actually use them. Under standard multimodal attention, downstream text and answer tokens can still directly access the original image tokens, allowing the model to answer with limited reliance on the latent intermediates. Thus, geometric supervision alone does not en-sure either differentiated geometry representations or effective use of the latent intermediates during answer learning. As summarized in Figure 1, we introduce GeoLatent , which improves both the geometric represen-tations learned by decomposed spatial latents and their role in subsequent spatial reasoning. Within GeoLatent, we first introduce Common–Residual Geometry Alignment (CR-GEO) , which mod-els the geometry shared across teacher features once at the set level and encourages different geome-try states to capture complementary residual geometry. This reduces redundant encoding and yields a more differentiated global geometry representation. We further develop routed optimization :training first jointly optimizes answer generation and geometric supervision, then uses a tempo-rary visual bottleneck to direct answer-relevant visual information through the latent intermediates, and finally restores full attention while retaining geometric supervision. This allows the model to recover flexible multimodal reasoning while preserving the spatial representations learned during routed training. Experimental results validate GeoLatent from multiple perspectives. Under identical initialization and batch order, replacing only GeoAnchor’s geometry alignment objective with CR-GEO increases the effective rank of the geometry representation from 1.00 to 3.87 . At the bottleneck stage, blocking downstream latent readout reduces accuracy on a fixed set of 128 direction questions from 89 .1% to 

25 .8% , showing that routed optimization makes answer prediction strongly dependent on the latent intermediates. After full visual access is restored, the differentiated geometry representation and the latent-mediated visual route remain available alongside direct image access; removing image content still causes substantial performance degradation, indicating that the final predictions remain strongly dependent on visual evidence. Overall, GeoLatent achieves 73 .0% on SPAR-Bench and 72 .1% on SPBench, improving over the reported GeoAnchor results by 4.6 and 2.4 points, respectively. Our main contributions are summarized as follows: • We analyze decomposed spatial latent reasoning and identify two remaining limitations: GeoAnchor’s geometry alignment can produce highly redundant global geometry repre-2sentations, while latents inserted into the reasoning trajectory can remain underused during answer learning. • We introduce GeoLatent , in which CR-GEO separates shared geometry from residual variation to learn more differentiated geometry representations, while routed optimiza-tion encourages answer-relevant visual information to pass through the latent intermediates during training. • Representation analyses, intervention experiments, and benchmark evaluations show that GeoLatent learns more differentiated geometry representations, establishes strong depen-dence on latent intermediates during routed training, and further improves spatial reasoning performance. 

2 RELATED WORK 

2.1 SPATIAL REASONING IN VISION -LANGUAGE MODELS 

Recent work improves spatial reasoning in VLMs mainly through spatial instruction tuning and progressive training (Chen et al., 2024; Cheng et al., 2024; Li et al., 2026d), or by incorporating explicit 2.5D/3D information such as depth, point clouds, and structured spatial representations (Wu et al., 2025a; Zhang et al., 2025a). These approaches provide stronger spatial supervision or geometric cues, but intermediate reasoning is still often expressed in discrete language, which can be insufficient for representing continuous spatial relations. More recently, GeoAnchor (Li et al., 2026c) moves beyond a uniform latent representation by decomposing 3D spatial information into complementary position, direction, and geometry latents with geometric supervision. 2.2 LATENT REASONING AND VISUAL INTERMEDIATES 

Latent reasoning replaces verbalized intermediate steps with hidden representations. CODI (Shen et al., 2025) compresses explicit chain-of-thought reasoning into a continuous latent space through self-distillation, while pause-token methods introduce additional internal computation without ex-plicit intermediate symbols (Goyal et al., 2024). In multimodal reasoning, methods such as Mirage and LVR use latent visual intermediates tied to task-specific visual targets, while Aurora introduces predefined perception tokens for visual quantities such as depth and bounding boxes (Yang et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026a; Bigverdi et al., 2025). LIVR (Li et al., 2026e) instead learns latent visual tokens without explicit intermediate supervision and uses visual bottlenecking to force task-relevant visual information through these tokens before answer prediction. RIS (Cui et al., 2026) identifies latent-trajectory collapse and answer bypass and addresses them with spatial-semantic grounding and progressive bottlenecking. Recent mechanistic evidence shows that latent-reasoning gains can reflect boundary markers, formatting, or attention patterns rather than slot content alone (Guo et al., 2026). Accordingly, GeoLatent evaluates both the geometric organization of decomposed spatial latents and their use during answer learning. 2.3 REPRESENTATION COLLAPSE 

Representation collapse has been widely studied in self-supervised representation learning (Caron et al., 2021; Zbontar et al., 2021; Bardes et al., 2022). Unlike the instance-agnostic latent-trajectory collapse studied in RIS (Cui et al., 2026), our redundancy arises within an explicitly supervised set of geometry states under coverage-based alignment: balanced utilization does not ensure that different states encode complementary information. GeoLatent addresses this setting with CR-GEO, representing shared geometry at the set level while encouraging individual geometry states to capture residual variation. We use effective rank to quantify the resulting representation diversity (Roy & Vetterli, 2007).

Figure 2 illustrates the overall framework. 3.1 PRELIMINARIES : D ECOMPOSED SPATIAL LATENTS 

GeoLatent follows GeoAnchor’s text–latent interleaved framework (Li et al., 2026c), where lan-guage tokens and spatial latents are generated within the same autoregressive reasoning trajectory. Each latent consists of continuous hidden states that are mapped back to the language-model input space by a latent projector, allowing them to condition subsequent generation. Separate geometry heads map the latent hidden states to their corresponding geometric target spaces. The decomposed latents encode complementary 3D information. POS and DIR represent local cues for object position and relative direction. Their hidden states are mean-pooled and mapped by sepa-rate linear heads to a camera-frame 3D position or unit direction vector, supervised with Smooth-ℓ1

(Girshick, 2015) and cosine losses, respectively. The corresponding targets are precomputed from Depth Anything v3 camera-frame back-projection (Lin et al., 2026). GEO instead represents global scene geometry through multiple latent states projected into the VGGT alignment space and super-vised against multi-scale pooled VGGT features (Wang et al., 2025a). Unlike POS and DIR, an individual GEO state is not assigned a predefined physical quantity; supervision is defined over the GEO set and the global teacher geometry. For training stage s, the overall objective is 

L(s) = LNTP + λ(s)local (Lpos + Ldir ) + λ(s)GEO LGEO , (1) where LNTP supervises the reasoning text and final answer, Lpos and Ldir supervise the local spatial latents, and LGEO denotes the supervision objective for the global GEO latents. In this paper, we denote GeoAnchor’s original coverage-based GEO objective as Lcov and our proposed CR-GEO ob-jective as LCR -GEO . Stage-specific weights and implementation details are given in Appendix B.5. Lcommon aligns the shared geometry once at the set level, while Lmatch and Lspec encourage dif-ferent GEO states to capture complementary residual geometry. The assignments are stop-gradient only in Lmatch .3.4 ROUTED OPTIMIZATION 

Geometric supervision alone does not ensure that the resulting spatial latents are used during answer learning. Under standard multimodal attention, downstream text and answer tokens can directly access image features and may therefore rely only weakly on the latent intermediates. Inspired by the visual bottlenecking strategy of LIVR (Li et al., 2026e), we introduce a three-stage routed optimization schedule for decomposed spatial latents. 

Joint. We first jointly optimize next-token prediction and geometric supervision under standard multimodal attention. This allows the decomposed spatial latents to develop together with reasoning and answer generation. 

Bottleneck. We then temporarily prevent question, intermediate-text, and answer queries from at-tending directly to image keys, while latent queries retain visual access. Visual information needed by subsequent reasoning and answer prediction must therefore pass through the generated spatial latents. Geometric supervision is retained during this stage to preserve their spatial representations. 

Recovery. Finally, we restore full visual attention while continuing geometric supervision. The model can again combine direct image access with the latent-mediated route learned during bottle-neck training, while retaining the differentiated geometric representations. Detailed routing masks, stage lengths, and loss weights are provided in Appendix B.5.

4.3 STRUCTURING THE LEARNED SPATIAL REPRESENTATION 

We assess global GEO differentiation using effective rank reff and assignment information Iassign :rank near one and Iassign ≈0 indicate highly redundant states with nearly identical teacher assign-ments. 

Global GEO organization. Figure 3 and Table 2(a) show the core CR-GEO comparison. The matched joint runs start from bit-identical initialization, see the same per-rank batch sequence, and differ only in the GEO objective. The original Lcov yields Iassign ≈0 and reff =1 .00 , whereas CR-GEO reaches 0.228 and 3.87 . This structural change is accompanied by a 1.29 -point three-benchmark gain, including a 1.85 -point ViewSpatial gain (paired McNemar p=0 .016 ). Importantly, the matched objective replacement improves all three benchmarks, so the increased representation diversity is accompanied by task gains rather than occurring only as a diagnostic change. At the separately trained recovered endpoints, the original objective remains rank one, while GeoLatent re-tains a differentiated representation with Iassign =0 .071 and reff =3 .62 , close to the teacher-residual effective rank of approximately 4.1.

Local POS/DIR geometry. The final POS/DIR latents remain quantitatively decodable: on 504 held-out records from 248 training-disjoint scenes, GeoLatent obtains 0.338 m POS error and 0.899 

independent-DIR cosine. Replacing only the RGB input with a different-scene image raises POS error from 0.389 to 1.474 m, showing that the decoded local geometry depends on the corresponding image.

Retaining geometric supervision improves the three-benchmark mean by 1.2 points without bottleneck training and by 2.6 points after bottleneck training, yielding a positive interaction of 1.4 points. GeoLatent addresses redundant GEO representations and underuse of decomposed spatial latents. CR-GEO separates shared from residual teacher geometry, while routed optimization temporarily directs visual answer learning through the latents before restoring full attention. B.1 DATA AND BENCHMARK CONSTRUCTION 

All training and evaluation samples are single-image examples. Local POS and DIR targets use the camera coordinate frame: Depth Anything v3 (DA3) depth is back-projected with camera in-trinsics and robust median aggregation inside each marked region, and DIR targets are normalized differences between the corresponding positions. The same construction is applied to every source. 

Spatial reasoning training mixture. Table 4 summarizes the 105,928-sample training mixture used by the main GeoLatent runs. It follows the source composition of the GeoAnchor spatial reasoning data: SPAR-derived ScanNet, ScanNet++, and Structured3D samples plus SpatialLadder single-image samples. In the released SPAR records, Structured3D spatial-imagination questions use sentence-format answers; we retain that released format for both corresponding buckets. 

Geometry supervision. In geometry-enabled configurations, records may contain local POS/DIR spans and a global GEO span. POS and DIR receive local geometric supervision when the corre-sponding objects are present, while the GEO span is aligned to VGGT teacher features. Following GeoAnchor, we use the final VGGT feature map and apply average pooling at resolutions {1, 2, 4},giving 12 + 2 2 + 4 2 = 21 teacher vectors per image. GeoAnchor’s GEO loss aligns the eight vectors within the GEO token to these teacher features with soft coverage and balance; GeoLatent replaces this alignment term with CR-GEO while leaving the teacher features and pooled resolution unchanged.
