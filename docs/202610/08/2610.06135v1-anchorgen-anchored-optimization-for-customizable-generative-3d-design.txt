# ABSTRACT 

Engineering design often starts from a 2D sketch that fixes style and proportions, yet the subsequent 3D shape optimization relies on learned generative priors to keep the geometry valid. However, these priors are agnostic to the sketch: while they admit a valid design by correcting a drifted proposal back to its training distribution, they often correct it towards the high-density region, ignoring the specified design. We introduce AnchorGen , a rectified-flow framework trained unconditionally on the concatenated shape and sketch latents of paired data. The learned manifold represents the joint distribution of shape-sketch pairs, so con-straining the sketch component restricts the iterate to the sub-manifold of shapes consistent with a target style. Since training employs no conditioning signal, the constraint is imposed at inference: gradient descent optimizes the shape latent to minimize a differentiable drag surrogate, while constraining the sketch latent to remain close to the target sketch via a token-wise cosine penalty. A single model thereby supports design-preserving optimization, dimensionally explicit design edits, and sketch-only synthesis.

# 1 INTRODUCTION 

Many 3D engineering design workflows start with a simple 2D sketch: A few contour lines often suffice to fix the proportions of a 3D shape together with the styling cues that carry its brand identity. This enables a designer to explore several potential directions in the time elsewise required to model a single one directly in 3D (Tovey et al., 2003; Lee et al., 2025a). This makes sketching a natural medium for early-stage brainstorming and exploration before committing time to full geometry. In-dustrial pipelines for CAD (Yang et al., 2026), product design (Cordier et al., 2013), and automotive 1arXiv:2610.06135v1 [cs.CV] 5 Oct 2026 Preprint styling (Nozawa et al., 2022) are organized accordingly, proceeding from sketching to 3D geometry and then to refinement. This has spurred considerable interest in lifting 2D sketches to 3D geometry, and large sketch- and image-conditioned generators can now handle complex geometries (Nozawa et al., 2022; Zheng et al., 2023; Li et al., 2025a). However, in engineering design, the initial 3D shape is not the deliver-able, but only the starting point of an optimization against a physical objective, such as minimizing aerodynamic drag or maximizing structural strength (You et al., 2026; Vatani et al., 2025). In most current pipelines (Zhan et al., 2025; Vatani et al., 2025), the sketch serves only to initialize the op-timization. Its influence does not extend beyond the starting point, as the subsequent trajectory is determined by the physical objective and by the prior that constrains the design to remain valid. In modern implementations (Seiler et al., 2026; You et al., 2026), the optimization is often performed with respect to latent vectors (Chen et al., 2025; Xiang et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2023) that pa-rameterize the shapes, whereas the physical objective is evaluated in the explicit shape space, either by CFD (Jasak et al., 2007) with an adjoint method (Othmer, 2014) or by a surrogate model (Wu et al., 2024) trained on a set of exemplars. Once the optimization starts, the latent vector can easily move away from the manifold of vectors representing valid shapes, resulting in implausible ones and meaningless values of the objective function, either because CFD fails or because the surrogate models are not trained for such shapes. This is referred to as drift in (Xiang et al., 2025; Seiler et al., 2026) and can be mitigated by projecting the candidates back into the region in which the models are valid, for example using guided generative algorithms (Lugmayr et al., 2022; Meng et al., 2022; Seiler et al., 2026). However, this amounts to using a prior trained on shapes alone to project onto the aggregate shape distribution. Even when the projection is guided by the physical objective, the correction itself is still applied by the aggregate shape prior, and thus favors generic shapes. In other words, the very mechanism that keeps the optimization valid is also what erases what the sketch specified: Such a prior is agnostic to design intent, it can certify that a design is valid, not deter-mine which valid design it should be. An optimizer asked to reduce the drag of a pickup truck may therefore succeed by returning a sedan or, as shown in Fig. 1(a), add an unintended rear wing while restyling the front bumper into that of a sports-car. What is missing is an interface for design intent. Parameterized deformation and learned parameter-controlled generators such as LAMP (Nehme et al., 2025) provide one through explicit control variables (Zhang et al., 2026b; Nehme et al., 2025), but they are bounded by a hand-designed pa-rameterization (Othmer, 2014; Masters et al., 2017) and tied to a few scalars, which makes them ill-suited to intent expressed as a roofline, a silhouette, or a tail treatment. Diffusion-based 3D mod-els (Seiler et al., 2026) are not subject to this restriction, since they learn the design space from data, but they come with no design interface in its place. The sketch that started the pipeline is a natural candidate for that interface (Tovey et al., 2003): The side view of a car captures features such as the roofline and the rear slant, which give the car its distinct look but can also severely affect drag. In this paper, we turn the sketch into such an interface by making it part of the prior rather than an input to it. Our AnchorGen framework, shown in Fig. 2, first learns latent representations for shapes and sketches, and then trains a single flow matching model (Lipman et al., 2022) over the concate-nation of the two, thereby learning the joint distribution of shapes and sketches. At inference time, we minimize the physical objective by steering the sampling trajectory of the flow: We optimize the flow’s intermediate states so that the decoded shape minimizes a surrogate estimate of the objective, while a penalty keeps the sketch component of the pair close to the latent vector of a target sketch in terms of cosine distance. Every candidate therefore remains a sample of the joint prior, and the penalty narrows that prior from the manifold of valid shapes to the subset consistent with the tar-get. As a result, corrections that would otherwise pull a drifting design toward generic shapes of the data now pull it toward that subset. The sketch acts as an anchor that persists throughout the optimization. Training is fully unconditional: Both modalities are noised and denoised together, so the model learns their correspondence instead of a conditional of one given the other. A conditional model must commit to its control interface before training, whereas ours makes it possible to choose what to constrain, how strongly, and alongside which objective without additional training. Our contributions are therefore as follows: • A prior over shape-sketch pairs. We train a single unconditional flow over the joint latent of a shape and its sketch, which turns the sketch from an initialization into a constraint that persists throughout the optimization. 2Preprint • Anchoring at inference time. We minimize a physical objective along this flow while constraining the sketch component to remain close to a target. The manifold correction is thereby directed to the sub-manifold of shapes consistent with the target sketch, instead of the high-density region of the shape distribution. • A single model for three design tasks. Because the anchor is imposed only at inference, a single trained model supports three different usages; optimization anchored to an existing 

sketch, optimization anchored to an edited sketch that prescribes a targeted design change, and synthesis from noise anchored to a sketch alone. We validate AnchorGen on two complementary benchmarks. ShapeNet (Chang et al., 2015) spans diverse vehicle types, enabling evaluation of whether optimized shapes remain within the original shape distribution against prior-based methods. DrivAerNet++ (Elrefaie et al., 2024) provides ex-plicit design parameters, enabling quantitative evaluation of how precisely customized designs are realized, with comparisons to conditioning-based methods. AnchorGen achieves the best results across all metrics on both benchmarks and further generalizes to unseen sparse freehand sketches. 

# 2 RELATED WORK 

2.1 CONTROLLABLE GENERATIVE MODELS FOR 3D S HAPES 

Controllable 3D generative models, most of which are now diffusion-based, combine a learned shape prior with user-specified evidence (Wang et al., 2025; Wei et al., 2026). How that evidence enters divides existing work into two families. Input-conditioned methods learn the constraint at training time: The prescribed signal is encoded and injected into the denoiser or velocity field at every sampling step (Ho & Salimans, 2022; Cheng et al., 2023; Xiang et al., 2026; Lee et al., 2025b; Cen et al., 2026). Such methods are efficient at inference, but the control signal is fixed at training time and cannot be optimized. Test-time steering methods instead leave the prior untouched and steer its inference trajectory by minimizing a differentiable loss evaluated on the running clean estimate (Dhariwal & Nichol, 2021; Chung et al., 2022; Song et al., 2023; Ben-Hamu et al., 2024). Such methods accommodate any differentiable objective without retraining and allow the constraint itself to be optimized, but require iterative sampling. In industrial 3D design, the generated shape must additionally satisfy physical objectives, such as minimizing aerodynamic drag, rather than only match a desired appearance. Performance-driven workflows therefore combine both mechanisms. They first use input conditioning to decode a latent vector into a 3D shape that conforms to the design intent, and then optimize a physical loss with respect to that vector (Morita et al., 2024; Vatani et al., 2025). Preserving the shape prior during this second stage can be done in two main ways. The first (Zhan et al., 2025) relies on a compact latent space Park et al. (2019) to keep optimization near the shape manifold, but this constraint weak-ens in high dimensions Chen et al. (2025), where the curse of dimensionality makes optimization more prone to drifting toward meaningless latents. The second is to let the gradient of the physical loss drive the generation, allowing the latent vector to drift away from the manifold of vectors that correspond to meaningful shapes (Liu et al., 2026), and to periodically re-noise and de-noise the can-didates to project them back onto that manifold (Lugmayr et al., 2022; Meng et al., 2022; Li et al., 2026; You et al., 2026; Seiler et al., 2026). The difficulty is that this projection is not guaranteed to return high-quality candidates. We overcome these limitations by jointly modeling the shapes and the corresponding sketches using a single latent vector for both. Such joint modeling has been explored before for shape generation purposes (Cheng et al., 2022; Bao et al., 2023; Li et al., 2025b). It has also been tried for image segmentation (Zhang et al., 2025) and other discriminative tasks (Zhang et al., 2026a). However, in all these methods, a variable is observed and the other is produced. Thus, the design focuses on architectures that support this exchange. In contrast, we optimize the shape and sketch jointly to minimize a physical loss, while constraining the sketch to remain close to a target. 2.2 SKETCH -B ASED 3D D ESIGN 

Sketches are a key medium for early-stage industrial design: A few contour and character lines si-multaneously fix the overall proportion of a shape and the styling details that carry brand identity. A designer can produce them much faster than a CAD parameterization, a photorealistic rendering, or a 3D scan (Tovey et al., 2003; Lee et al., 2025a; Huang et al., 2026). However, design is only one of the applications that have motivated work on sketches, and the algorithms that lift a 2D draw-ing to a 3D shape have largely been developed as solutions to a general-purpose inverse problem rather than as design tools. Early work addressed the inherent ambiguity of that problem either by assuming that drawn lines correspond to specific shape features (Malik & Maydan, 1989; Igarashi et al., 2006), or by restricting the class of representable 3D shapes (Leclerc & Fischler, 1992; Lipson & Shpitalni, 1996; Cordier et al., 2013; Jung et al., 2015; Dvorožˇ nák et al., 2020). Deep learning brought more expressive models and better generalization (Lun et al., 2017; Delanoy et al., 2018; Jin et al., 2020; Nozawa et al., 2022).More recently, diffusion models have been conditioned on sketches through view-aware attention (Zheng et al., 2023), and autoregressive models enable in-teractive sketch-based mesh generation and editing (Li et al., 2025a). These methods target faithful reconstruction or plausible content creation, and are evaluated as such. Few address engineering directly, e.g., retrieving sketch-matched shapes for aerodynamic evaluation (Elrefaie et al., 2025) or inferring CAD part articulation from sketched arrows (Yang et al., 2026). On the other hand, physics-aware 3D generation methods (Seiler et al., 2026; Zhan et al., 2025) use the design input only to initialize the optimization, so the final geometry after optimization may reach the physical target while losing the intended styling. The few methods that retain the sketch during optimization (Guillard et al., 2021; You et al., 2026) tend to enforce strict fidelity to it: the same fixed sketch is injected at every iteration, e.g. as a conditioning input or through classifier-free guidance, instead of evolving together with the shape as the optimization proceeds. This is limiting because it does not allow a designer to specify a specific style while allowing small deviations to increase performance. AnchorGen strikes a happy medium between these two extremes. 

# 3 ANCHOR GEN 

Our goal is to generate a 3D shape S that minimizes a numerical physical objective such as aero-dynamic drag, while preserving the original design intent expressed as a target image sketch I.

AnchorGen encodes shapes and sketches into latent vectors of the same size and trains a single flow matching model over their concatenation, thereby modeling the joint distribution of shapes and 4Preprint sketches. At inference time, it minimizes the objective through that flow: The optimization variable is an intermediate state of the generative ODE, and the resulting pair it generates is scored by the objective on the decoded shape together with the distance from its sketch component to the target. Every iterate is therefore an output of the joint prior, restricted to the shapes that matching the target sketch. Fig. 2 summarizes these three stages, all of which operate in latent space, which keeps the dimensionality low and makes both the flow model and the optimization loop tractable.

# 4 EXPERIMENTS 

We evaluate the three modes of operation intro-duced in Sec. 1 using a single trained model, us-ing the setup of Sec. 4.1. In Sec. 4.2, we demon-strate that anchoring to an existing sketch sup-presses manifold drift and preserves design intent, as shown in Fig. 1(a). In Sec. 4.3, we further show that a shape attribute can be added or changed by anchoring to an edited sketch, as shown in Fig. 1(b). Finally, in Sec. 4.4, we demonstrate synthesis from scratch given only a sketch, as shown in Fig. 5. ShapeNet features a diverse inter-class design space and about 50% of the baselines suc-cessfully manage a 30% drag reduction. On DrivAerNet++, which is less diverse, about 50% of the baselines deliver a 20% drag reduction. Thus, these reduction numbers are the largest ones for which we can make a meaningful comparison and the ones we use in this section. We provide results for lesser values of the drag reduction in Appendix G. 4.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

Datasets. We use two complementary vehicle datasets: ShapeNet (Chang et al., 2015), which emphasizes geometric diversity across car types, and DrivAerNet++ (Elrefaie et al., 2024), which provides an explicit parameterized design space with high-fidelity aerodynamics. On ShapeNet, we follow the setup of (Seiler et al., 2026), including its surrogate model for drag evaluation. On DrivAerNet++, where every design comes with explicit length, width and height parameters and high-fidelity CFD annotations, we train the shape prior and the surrogate model on its training split. For every shape, we render a normal map with a fixed orthographic camera and prompt Qwen (Wu et al., 2025), which yields a sketch consistent with the silhouette, proportions and part semantics of the shape. Appendix B gives further details on data preprocessing and sketch generation. 

Baselines. We compare against the two families of Sec. 2. Prior-based methods steer a shape-only prior at test time and differ in how the physical objective is descended: gradient descent (GD) (Zhan et al., 2025), Flow Matching Guidance (FMG) (Ho & Salimans, 2022), D-Flow (Ben-Hamu et al., 2024), ICTM (Zhang et al., 2024), SGO (Chen et al., 2026), and FCSO (Seiler et al., 2026). We eval-uate them on both benchmarks. Conditioning-based methods instead learn a conditional distribution 

P (Y | X) of shapes given sketches, making the sketch an explicit input at inference time, and differ in how the sketch injects a direction into the optimization: through plain input conditioning (Du et al., 2025), or through classifier-free guidance (CFG) with guidance-weight schedules (Xi et al., 2024) or modified update rules (Sadat et al., 2024; Fan et al., 2025; Saini et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2026). Since they require a sketch that encodes a design intent, we evaluate them on DrivAerNet++ only. Appendix C lists all variants and implementation details.

We explore two different ways to preserve style and avoid manifold drift when minimizing the drag of an existing vehicle. One can either use the actual sketch of the original vehicle as the anchor or use the sketch of a lower-drag vehicle of the same type to guide the optimization in the desired direction. This mirrors design practice, where a designer knows, for instance, that moderately lowering the roof usually improves drag. We test this on ShapeNet, which is highly diverse, featuring all kinds of different cars ranging from compact sedans to trucks. As a result, optimization can easily alter the vehicle type or produce distorted shapes, making ShapeNet a sensitive probe for manifold drift. 

Anchoring to the vehicle’s own sketch. Fig. 3 compares our results against the baselines at 10% 

drag reduction. Green boxes mark examples of identity features of the initial shape, such as the front bumper. Since all baselines rely on a shape prior alone to correct the geometry, none of them reliably retains these features: GD and D-Flow smooth them out, ICTM and SGO distort them, and FCSO even grows parts the source never had, such as a rear wing. AnchorGen , in contrast, retains them in all three examples. See Appendix F for quantitative results and ablations.

Dimensional edits. Additional experiments in the Appendix further demonstrate that the improvements stem from the direction supplied by the anchor rather than from the prior alone. The sketch-to-drag ratio λ trades adherence to the target sketch against drag reduction: a larger weight brings the result closer to an exogenous target, whereas anchoring too strongly to the source’s own sketch confines the search and slows the drag descent (Appendix F). Edits obtained with shape-prior-only correction collapse towards the high-density region of the data, whereas ours spread along the directions prescribed by the target sketches (Appendix H). AnchorGen scales with parameter count, yet capacity alone does not close the gap: our smallest model, with 14 M parameters, already outperforms an 82 M-parameter FCSO on all seven metrics of Table 2 (Appendix H). 

# 5 CONCLUSION 

We introduced AnchorGen , which turns the design sketch from an initialization into an anchor that persists throughout physics-driven shape optimization. A single unconditional flow over shape-sketch pairs, constrained only at inference, directs the corrections that keep a design valid toward the shapes the sketch specifies rather than the high-density region of the data. Thus, one model supports optimization anchored to an existing or edited sketch, and synthesis from a sketch alone. # B DATASET 

Data Preprocessing. ShapeNet: We use the car category of ShapeNet (Chang et al., 2015), which spans a wide range of body styles and topologies but provides no design parameterization, and therefore probes how well a shape prior generalizes when the manifold of valid shapes is broad and unstructured. We adopt the same data and split as FCSO (Seiler et al., 2026): meshes are made watertight, aligned and normalized to [−1, 1] 3, and the corresponding drag coefficients are obtained with OpenFOAM (Jasak et al., 2007). This yields 836 shapes for training the shape prior and the drag-conditioned flow matching model, on top of which we report the optimization process on 100 

test cases. 

DrivAerNet++ (Elrefaie et al., 2024) is built by morphing a family of parametric DrivAer models, so every design is fully specified by a set of design parameters covering overall dimensions such as length, width and roof height as well as discrete configuration choices such as the rear-end type and the underbody treatment. We discard the detailed-underbody designs, whose variation lies in the chassis structure rather than in the exterior shape we optimize, and retain 4,165 designs, partitioned into 3,051 training, 555 validation and 559 test samples. The shape prior and the surrogate model are trained on the training split. 

Sketch generation. All sketches in this work are synthetic and derived from 3D meshes through a fixed pipeline. We place each mesh under a fixed orthographic camera convention and render a 1024 × 768 normal map, using consistently oriented cell normals transformed into camera co-ordinates, with each component mapped linearly from [−1, 1] to RGB. Depending on the exper-iment we render a single canonical viewpoint or several complementary ones; only the normal map is passed downstream. Each normal map is converted into a black-and-white line drawing 16 Preprint with Qwen/Qwen-Image-Edit-2511 1 (Wu et al., 2025) in image-edit mode, the normal ren-der being the only image input. Prior-injection baselines. This family of baselines compares the mechanisms by which a shape prior supplies a direction to the optimization, with particular attention to controllability: the sketch strength that each mechanism can actually attain in practice. All variants use the same frozen en-coders and the same target sketch, and differ only in how that sketch reaches the velocity field. 

Input conditioning. The simplest choice is to fix the conditioning signal at training time rather than to intervene at inference. The conditional flow diffuses only the shape latent and concatenates the clean sketch latent along the channel dimension, so the model directly learns p(zshape | zsketch ).With noise at t = 0 and data at t = 1 , we set xt = (1 − t)ϵ + tz and train with E∥vθ (xt, t, c ) − (z −

ϵ)∥22. Side, front, and rear views use three separately trained models sharing the same backbone and optimization setting. Because the condition is baked into the learned field, its influence cannot be adjusted after training; no condition dropout is used here. 

Classifier-free guidance. The remaining baselines move the intervention to inference time. An otherwise identical model is retrained with the entire sketch latent zeroed with probability 0.1, which lets a single network produce both a conditional velocity vc = vθ (x, t, c ) and an unconditional one 

vu = vθ (x, t, 0) ; their difference then becomes an explicit, tunable direction. All variants below reuse these same checkpoints and none trains a dedicated network, so they differ purely in how the two branches are combined. • Constant, Linear, and Exponential schedules (Xi et al., 2024). These vary when the guid-ance acts rather than what it points to, isolating the temporal weighting of one and the same CFG residual. All three apply v = vu + ω(t)( vc − vu) with ω = s, ω(t) = 2 ts ,and ω(t) = se t/(e − 1) respectively; the constant schedule gives a time-homogeneous reference, the linear one strengthens the condition as the clean endpoint is approached, and the exponential one redistributes weight nonlinearly through a convex time profile. We use 

s = 2 throughout. • APG (Sadat et al., 2024). Rather than rescaling the residual as a whole, APG filters its geometry, controlling the magnitude and the direction of the update separately. Clean-endpoint estimates ˆx1,• = x + (1 − t)v• are formed from both branches; their difference is accumulated with momentum β = 0 .5, clipped at norm 5, and reduced to the component orthogonal to ˆx1,u (η = 0 ) before being converted back to a velocity. • Rectified-CFG++ (Saini et al., 2025). Its distinguishing feature is a half-step look-ahead: the correction is recomputed at a locally advanced rectified-flow state instead of taken from the branch difference at the current state. The conditional–unconditional correction is eval-uated at an Euler midpoint with strength α(t) = λmax tγ , where λmax = γ = 1 , so its strength is governed entirely by these two parameters rather than by the generic guidance scale. • CFG-Zero ⋆ (Fan et al., 2025). Instead of a prescribed schedule, the coefficient here is state-dependent, calibrated to the instantaneous alignment between the two branches. The unconditional branch is rescaled by s∗ = ⟨vc, v u⟩/(∥vu∥22+ϵ), giving v = (1 −s)s∗vu+sv c

with s = 2 , and the first step of each forward trajectory returns zero velocity. • SMC (Wang et al., 2026). Guidance is cast as state feedback that depends not only on the current branch gap but also on how it changed since the previous ODE step, which sets SMC apart from the memoryless algebraic extrapolation of standard CFG. DrivAerNet++. E.1 AUTOENCODER MODEL 

Shape branch. We adopt a VecSet-style neural field autoencoder (Zhang et al., 2023). The en-coder Enc S takes a surface point cloud P ∈ R2048 ×3, selects 256 anchor points by farthest point sampling, and lets the corresponding tokens cross-attend to the full point set at width 512 , yielding a set-structured latent zS ∈ R256 ×8. The decoder Dec S lifts zS from 8 to 512 channels, refines it with 12 self-attention layers, and cross-attends it with arbitrary query coordinates to produce a continuous signed distance field f (p | zS ); an explicit mesh is recovered by marching cubes on a 

256 3 grid over [−1, 1] 3. We reuse the released checkpoint of Seiler et al. (2026) without retraining.

E.2 GRADIENT COMBINATION 

The objective O and the anchor loss Lanchor frequently conflict, and naively summing their gradi-ents lets whichever term happens to have the larger magnitude dominate the update. We therefore follow the multi-objective optimization literature (Désidéri, 2012; Sener & Koltun, 2018) and com-bine the two at the level of gradients rather than losses. The objective gradient is first projected onto the shape-token block, since O is defined only through Dec S and carries no meaningful signal on the sketch tokens. Both gradients are then normalized to unit length, so that their relative influence is governed by the weights rather than by their raw magnitudes, and mixed with coefficients sum-ming to one. A single scalar exposed to the outer loop thus controls the trade-off between the two objectives.

# I GENERATION FROM SCRATCH 

Setting. We compare against PhysGen (You et al., 2026) using its official code and released weights. Each PhysGen cycle has two stages: the clean Dora latent is first refined with 30 Adam steps (learning rate 5 × 10 −3, proximity regularizer of weight 10 −3 toward the cycle’s starting la-tent), then re-noised to σ ≈ 0.25 and denoised with 75 Euler steps of sketch-conditioned sampling (CFG scale 5)., and we run 60 cycles.
