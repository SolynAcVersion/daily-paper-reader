Abstract 

Modern image editing models can satisfy a text instruction while breaking the physics of the edited scene. A new object may cast no shadow, a mirror may fail to reflect visible geometry, or an object may float above a surface that should support it. We study physical plausibility diagnosis, detecting whether an edited image violates scene physics, naming the violation type, localizing the affected region, and explaining the failure in language. We introduce a counterfactual benchmark whose controlled synthetic component uses Mitsuba 3 to generate 5,500 images from 500 scene families. Each family contains one clean image and ten matched violations involving shadows, reflection, support, surface response, and occlusion. The renderer pipeline provides category labels, affected-region masks and boxes, scene metadata, and explanation targets. We use LLaVA-1.5-7B, Qwen2.5-VL-7B, and InternVL3.5-8B as diagnostic baselines rather than proposed methods. On a 1,650-image synthetic test set, the adapted baselines reach 64.0–67.8% category macro-F1 on standard held-out scenes. For LLaVA-1.5-7B, category macro-F1 falls from 64.0% on the standard split to 40.8% under intervention shift. This gap shows that high in-distribution accuracy partly reflects cues tied to rendering and counterfactual construction. 

1 Introduction 

Vision models can recognize objects, scenes, and visual relations with high accuracy, but judging physical consistency requires a different form of understanding. An image may appear realistic while containing evidence that conflicts with basic scene constraints. A cast shadow may disagree with the apparent illumination, a reflective surface may omit an object that should be visible, or an object may appear unsupported by the surface beneath it. Such failures are relevant to generated and edited imagery, but they also provide a useful test of whether visual models reason correctly about physical relations rather than surface appearance alone. Evaluating this capability is difficult because physical inconsistencies are often sparse, local, and hard to annotate. Real images rarely provide ground-truth labels for the physical rule that is violated or the image region that provides the relevant evidence. Moreover, a high score on a synthetic test set does not necessarily show that a model has learned the intended physical relation. We study this problem as grounded physical consistency. Given a static RGB image, the task is to determine whether its visible evidence is consistent with a set of image-observable physical constraints; if it is not, the task is to identify the inconsistency and localize the affected region. We focus on constraints involving illumination and shadows, reflection, support and contact, surface response, and depth ordering. Rather than treating physical consistency as a single binary judgment, the benchmark records the physical constraint, the affected region, and the scene parameters used to construct each example. To obtain controlled supervision, we generate paired physical counterfactuals from rendered scenes. Each physically consistent scene is paired with variants in which a targeted physical relation is changed while the remaining scene configuration is held fixed. This design provides category labels, bounding boxes, masks, and physical metadata without requiring manual annotation. The paired construction also lets us ask a harder question than in-distribution classification: does a model recognize the physical constraint, or does it learn shortcuts tied to the renderer and intervention procedure? Our evaluation therefore covers both recognition and generalization. In addition to held-out scene instances, we consider changes in scene appearance and violation-generation mechanisms, which provide stronger tests of whether the learned cues transfer beyond the conditions used to construct the training data. We used a filtered subset of PICA-100K for adaptation and reserve disjoint PICABench samples for evaluation on physics-aware image editing. We provide renderer-derived supervision for affected regions, covering both introduced visual cues and expected locations of missing cues. 3. We define evaluation settings that separate held-out scene recognition from appearance and intervention shifts, allowing us to test whether models learn physical constraints or rendering shortcuts. 4. 2 Related Work 

Physical reasoning in vision and vision-language models. Recent benchmarks have examined whether vision-language models understand physical properties, object relations, and scene dynamics. PhysBench evaluates physical-world understanding across object properties, spatial relations, scene-level reasoning, and dynamic events Chow et al. [2025]. Other diagnostic benchmarks have shown that strong multimodal models can still fail on visually grounded reasoning and related forms of multimodal understanding Guan et al. [2024], Li et al. [2025], Yue et al. [2024]. These works motivate dedicated evaluation of physical understanding, but they generally cover broad reasoning tasks rather than controlled image-level physical inconsistencies with intervention-associated spatial supervision. SPR-128K focuses more specifically on spatial plausibility reasoning with multimodal language models Hu et al. [2026], while PhyDetEx studies the detection and explanation of physical implausibility in generated videos Wang et al. [2025b]. Our focus is complementary: we study static images through paired physical counterfactuals and evaluate whether models can identify and spatially ground the violated physical constraint. 

Physical consistency and image editing evaluation. Physical realism has become an important consideration in image generation and editing. Prior work has shown that generated images can violate projective geometry, object-shadow relations, and other physical constraints even when their overall appearance is realistic Sarkar et al. [2024]. Physics-aware editing benchmarks extend this observation by evaluating whether an edit preserves physical effects associated with the modified content. PICABench is particularly relevant to this study. It evaluates physically realistic image editing through region-grounded questions covering optical, mechanical, and state-transition effects Pu et al. [2026]. Re-Edit considers image editing through a reasoning-aware evaluation framework Ding et al. [2026], while PhyEditBench studies physics-aware editing using real-world examples and a structured physical taxonomy Guo et al. [2026]. These works show that instruction following and perceptual quality alone are insufficient for eval-uating edited images. Our benchmark addresses this gap by isolating physical constraints through paired counterfactual rendering, providing exact intervention metadata and spatial supervision, and supports controlled tests of whether model performance transfers beyond the rendering procedure used to construct the data. We therefore select PICABench as the external evaluation source for physics-aware edited images. 

Manipulation detection and grounded analysis. Forgery detectors localize image changes using visual artifacts, physical cues, or multimodal explanations Wang et al. [2022], Guillaro et al. [2023], 3Johnson and Farid [2005], Kee et al. [2013], Xu et al. [2025]. Our task differs from authenticity detection: it tests a named physical constraint and grounds the region affected by a controlled intervention. Additional related work is summarized in Appendix A. 

Synthetic counterfactual supervision and benchmark design. Synthetic rendering controls geometry, illumination, materials, and visibility, but may introduce renderer- or intervention-specific shortcuts. We retain paired counterfactuals for exact labels and spatial supervision, then separate appearance shifts from changes in the intervention mechanism. The latter is the stricter test of whether a model recognizes the intended relation. 

3 Task Definition 

We focus on grounded physical consistency in a static RGB image x. The task is to determine whether the visible scene evidence is consistent with a set of image-observable physical constraints, identify the violated constraint when an inconsistency is present, and localize its affected region. We write the model output as 

fθ (x) = (ˆ y, ˆc, ˆr, ˆe), (1) where ˆy ∈ { 0, 1} denotes physical consistency, ˆc denotes the predicted physical constraint, ˆr is a normalized bounding box for the affected region, and ˆe is a natural-language text explanation of the inconsistency. Physically consistent images receive the clean label and no violation category. The benchmark covers constraints related to illumination and shadows, reflection, support and contact, surface response, and depth ordering. These constraints are selected because their relevant evidence can be represented visually under the controlled scene conditions used in the benchmark. We do not treat the task as determining whether an image is physically possible under every unobserved scene configuration. Instead, the goal is to evaluate whether the visible cues are mutually consistent with the physical assumptions defined for each benchmark group. The ten labels are fine-grained sub-types within five physical families. Some pairs are intentionally close, including wrong shadow direction versus shadow illumination inconsistency and floating object versus impossible occlusion. Their operational boundaries and shortcut risks are listed in Appendix 4; we do not assume that the labels are independent. The spatial target localizes the region affected by the intervention. For an introduced cue, such as a displaced shadow, it covers the anomalous region; for a missing cue, it covers the region where that cue should appear. This target need not contain every contextual cue required to diagnose the violation. We therefore distinguish affected-region localization from the stricter problem of complete evidence grounding in Section 4. 

4 Counterfactual Physical Consistency Benchmark 

Controlled scene construction. Our evaluation uses multiple image sources; here we describe the Mitsuba 3 pipeline used to generate the controlled subset. It contains 500 scene families and 5,500 images: one clean correct image and ten counterfactual images per family. Mitsuba 3 Jakob et al. [2022] scenes are deterministic from a recorded seed. Families or categories vary the room, object geometry, pose, material, illumination, and camera. We render 500 × 500 images; the full set takes about ten hours on one NVIDIA A100 GPU with ten CPU cores and 50 GB of system memory. Renderer and sampling details appear in Appendix C. Each label therefore has 300 training, 50 validation, and 150 test images, with 50 test images in each suite. Models may use only the training split for adaptation and the validation split for selection. All three test suites use a fixed prompt, parser, and checkpoint. The intervention-shift macro-F1 is the primary result for the question posed in the title. 

Matched physical counterfactuals. The subset covers wrong shadow direction, missing cast shadow, floating objects, shadow illumination inconsistency, missing reflection, reflection angle mismatch, incorrect specular highlight, wrong reflected object identity, impossible occlusion order, and missing contact shadow. Violations are produced either by intervening on scene parameters or by compositing auxiliary renders in linear floating-point RGB radiance. Appearance and intervention shifts. The appearance shift partition uses object geometries, ma-terial configurations, and lighting combinations that are disjoint from training while retaining the same category definitions and counterfactual mechanisms. The intervention-shift partition instead matches the training appearance distribution but holds out the construction mechanism for each semantic violation. For example, a shadow-direction violation can be generated by changing illu-mination geometry or by transferring an auxiliary-render shadow; only one mechanism is observed during training. Across both shifts, scene families remain disjoint, categories are balanced, and clean/counterfactual pairs share the same nuisance variables. We additionally match affected-region area and counterfactual effect size across mechanisms to reduce low-level artifact cues. Composited counterfactuals can still contain boundary halos, small color changes, or other editing cues. We therefore treat intervention shift as a required result rather than an optional robustness test. We also audit difference images and report performance by construction mechanism. These checks do not prove that every shortcut has been removed; they make the most obvious shortcuts visible in the evaluation. 

Renderer-derived annotations. For each pair, the pipeline derives its clean/violated status, cat-egory, procedural scene parameters, intervention metadata, a templated physical explanation, an affected-region mask, and its tight axis-aligned bounding box. A semantic visibility integrator marks pixels whose primary ray intersects named geometry, and a cast-shadow integrator marks visible receiver points for which the target object blocks the point light. Other categories use render differ-ences or known compositing regions. These geometry-derived passes avoid extracting spatial targets by thresholding the final RGB image. We add an intervention mask containing pixels directly changed by construction, from an evidence mask, containing all visual evidence needed for diagnosis. The stored affected_mask represents the former; the two coincide for some categories but not necessarily for cases such as a floating object, where the air gap is diagnostically important. We therefore describe current spatial supervision as affected-region localization and do not assume that it is a complete evidence annotation. 

Validation. Generation validates object and cast-shadow visibility, mask and bounding-box area, mask fragmentation, and large boundary changes around composited regions. Sampling is determin-istic with respect to the recorded scene seed, and we group all samples from the same underlying scene family when constructing data partitions. Counterfactual construction occurs in linear radiance before image conversion. In the final release, aligned raw images and masks are retained, while independently denoised outputs are reserved for presentation. Output metadata will record the Mit-suba and Dr.Jit versions, renderer variant, hardware backend, render seed, integrator and sampling configuration, image-processing settings, and generator version. 5PICA-20K adaptation subset. We select 20,000 valid, visibly grounded examples from PICA-100K. Source videos and edit identities are disjoint between adaptation and evaluation. Selection fields and reconstruction details appear in Appendix C. Additional clean/counterfactual pairs for every category appear in Appendix B. They show variation across objects and camera settings and make residual rendering or compositing cues easier to audit. 

5 Benchmark Models and Adaptation 

We evaluate vision and vision-language models as benchmark baselines rather than proposing a new model architecture. The probe set includes LLaVA-1.5-7B Liu et al. [2023], Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct Bai et al. [2025], and InternVL3.5-8B Wang et al. [2025a]. These models provide different visual encoders, image-resolution policies, and language backbones at a similar scale. We evaluate the pretrained models and adapt each one with LoRA Hu et al. [2022]. The adaptation is a common measurement tool; it is not a proposed learning method. Each training example contains an image and an instruction requesting a structured physical-consistency diagnosis. The target specifies consistency, the physical constraint when applicable, the affected region, and an explanation. Prompt tokens are excluded from the training loss so that optimization is applied to the diagnostic response. The spatial output is a normalized (xmin , y min , x max , y max ) box. Renderer masks define and vali-date the target box but are not model outputs. Coordinate conversion and validation are given in Appendix C. We train a synthetic-only model for zero-shot PICABench transfer and a mixed-source model that additionally uses PICA-20K. The latter converts each grounded question–answer pair and its box into the same structured response format. The two variants are evaluated separately to distinguish transfer from adaptation. All three models use the same data, response schema, validation rule, and three-seed protocol. Hyperparameters, preprocessing, and adapter targets are listed in Appendix C. 

6 Experiments 

Setup. We separate four evaluation settings. The standard held-out split measures in-distribution generalization to unseen scene identities. The appearance shift holds out object geometries, materials, and lighting configurations while preserving the intervention mechanisms. The intervention shift renders the same semantic violations using mechanisms not observed during training while matching the scene-appearance distribution. Finally, PICABench evaluation uses its native region-grounded questions rather than forcing its broader physical taxonomy into our ten synthetic categories. We report both zero-shot transfer from synthetic-only training and adaptation with PICA-20K on a disjoint held-out subset. Model interpretation and causal analysis are outside the scope of this benchmark study. The combined synthetic test contains 1,650 images from 150 unseen scene families: 550 images in each of the standard, appearance-shift, and intervention-shift suites. The model generates structured text, from which we parse the binary decision, one of ten violation categories or clean, the affected region, and the explanation. We report binary accuracy, binary balanced accuracy, binary macro-F1, both binary class recalls, 11-class accuracy and macro-F1, box IoU, invalid-output rate, and explanation correctness. Invalid or missing spatial outputs receive zero localization score and also count as invalid. Adapted results average three random seeds. A human reference study uses three raters on the same stratified image set without renderer metadata.

Standard held-out results. Zero-shot CLIP and LLaVA perform poorly on the 11-class task. LLaVA also over-predicts violations, as shown by its 2.0% clean recall. Adaptation makes the labels learnable for all three VLMs, with Qwen2.5-VL giving the strongest category macro-F1 (67.8%). This result alone does not show physical reasoning; that claim depends on the shift results in Table 3. The 90.9% majority accuracy follows directly from the ten violated images for every clean image. It is therefore not evidence of useful detection. Balanced accuracy, binary macro-F1, and the separate clean and violated recalls are the primary binary measures; raw binary accuracy is retained for completeness. 

Per-category results. Appendix D gives a category breakdown and confusion matrix for the earlier 1,650-image development pilot. It exposes complete confusion within two deliberately close pairs: shadow direction versus illumination consistency, and impossible occlusion versus floating. These results motivate reporting both broad detection and fine-grained categories. 

Generalization beyond held-out scenes. Table 3 organizes the measurements needed to distinguish constraint learning from procedural shortcuts. LLaVA loses 11.6 category-F1 points under appearance shift and 23.2 points under intervention shift. The larger intervention gap indicates substantial sensitivity to how a violation is constructed. PICABench further separates synthetic transfer from adaptation to real edited images. 

Affected-region and explanation evaluation. We compute box IoU between the normalized pre-dicted box and the tight box derived from the renderer’s affected_mask . This measures localization of the intervention-associated region, not complete causal evidence grounding. For PICABench, we normalize its (x, y, w, h ) question boxes using the edited image dimensions and evaluate against the box associated with each native question. Malformed responses, missing fields, non-positive or out-of-range coordinates, and unrecognized categories contribute to the invalid-output rate; failed spatial predictions receive zero IoU. Explanation correctness is assessed independently of wording using a three-level rubric: incorrect, partially correct, or correct identification of the violated relation and its visible consequence. We report the fraction that receive a majority “correct” rating from three raters. Agreement is substantial (Fleiss’ κ = 0 .78 ). Human performance uses a category-balanced subset of 220 images. 7 Discussion, Limitations, and Ethics 

The standard and shift results show that a VLM can fit several rendered violation labels, but does not retain this accuracy under controlled changes. Categories with direct visual evidence are easier than closely related shadow and geometry sub-types. This is evidence of benchmark learnability, not of general physical reasoning. The CLIP zero-shot result is instructive: text-image similarity alone performs poorly on the standard split (1.9% category macro-F1). Adaptation is necessary, but the 23.2-point intervention gap shows that it still learns cues tied to counterfactual construction. PICA-20K adaptation improves external box IoU by 12.1 points and QA correctness by 10.5 points, but both remain well below the synthetic standard split. Affected-region masks provide a reproducible localization target but do not always capture every cue needed for causal diagnosis. We therefore report them as intervention-associated localization and evaluate explanations separately. PICABench complements this controlled target with region-grounded questions on edited images, although its broader taxonomy is not directly interchangeable with our ten synthetic categories. The synthetic construction also leaves visible risks. In particular, removed contact shadows can create boundary or halo cues, and some auxiliary-render composites may change more than the intended relation. The paired design and area matching reduce these cues but cannot remove them completely. For this reason, the intervention-shift result and mechanism-level breakdowns are central to the main claim. A high violation score should not be treated as proof that an image was forged, generated, or edited with malicious intent. We position the system as a diagnostic tool for editing pipelines and dataset auditing, not as an authenticity oracle. 

8 Conclusion 

We introduced a counterfactual benchmark for grounded physical consistency. Its 500 scene families provide 5,500 clean and violated images with category, region, metadata, and language supervision. Adapted VLMs learn the standard split, but LLaVA category macro-F1 drops from 64.0% to 40.8% when the intervention mechanism changes. The answer to the title question is therefore mixed: the model learns useful physical categories, but also relies strongly on construction-specific cues. External adaptation helps, yet a large transfer gap remains. Future work will study internal model representations, causal interventions on model features, and the faithfulness of generated explanations. Those questions are outside this study.

A Additional Related Work 

Image manipulation detectors use high-frequency features, object consistency, and diffusion priors to detect and localize changes Wang et al. [2022], Guillaro et al. [2023], Li et al. [2024], Yu et al. [2024]. Physical approaches instead test illumination or shadow consistency Johnson and Farid [2005], Kee et al. [2013]. FakeShield, ForgeryGPT, and ForenX add grounded language explanations Xu et al. [2025], Zhang et al. [2026], Tan et al. [2025]. These methods address authenticity or manipulation. Our benchmark instead labels the physical relation changed by a controlled intervention.
