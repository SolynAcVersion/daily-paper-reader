# Only Project Once: Projection-Adaptive Loss for Exact Constraint Satisfaction 

## Tim Aebersold 1∗ Soheyl Massoudi 1 Mark Fuge 11ETH Zürich 

§ https://github.com/IDEALLab/projection-adaptive-loss 

Abstract 

Precise constraint satisfaction is a prerequisite to deploying learned models in many areas, motivating methods that repair raw neural predictions with a repair procedure. Current methods unroll multiple repair steps in training and softly penalize constraint violations that remain after the unroll. This is compute- and memory-intensive, lacks robustness when the repair fails to converge, and surrenders most of the constraint satisfaction work to the repair. Our central finding is that, contrary to common practice, a single detached projection step suffices in training. We accomplish this with a Projection-Adaptive Loss (PAL), which uses the constraint residual after this single step to adaptively weigh constraint penalties on the raw prediction. In experiments, PAL is the only method that retains virtually perfect feasibility on extremely nonlinear constraints, and matches or outperforms current methods on synthetic and engineering benchmarks. Because it only requires a single detached projection step, PAL trains 2.5 × faster than the canonical repair-based method (DC3) on its own ACOPF benchmark. PAL can also be trained when constraints are expensive to evaluate (e.g., via neural surrogates), a setting where current unrolled methods are memory-intractable. 

# 1 Introduction 

Precise enforcement of hard constraints remains a key challenge in many applications of machine learning. For example, in atmospheric and fluids modeling, neural surrogates must adhere to conservation laws for stable roll-out over long time horizons (Watt-Meyer et al., 2023; Krishnapriyan et al., 2021). In power systems and robotics, learned policies must reliably conform to operational or safety limits to be deployed (Pan et al., 2023; Brunke et al., 2022), and in mechanical and aerospace design, generated designs must not violate stress or aerodynamic limits (Hoyer et al., 2019; Bouhlel et al., 2020). Current methods that enforce constraint satisfaction can typically be divided into two categories. Penalty methods use fixed or adaptive constraint weights in the training loss and typically do not exactly satisfy constraints. More recent methods apply a repair procedure to raw model predictions to ensure constraints are satisfied. In this latter category, the current predominant approach to train these methods is to unroll multiple repair steps per training iteration. Any residual violation at the end of the unroll is then softly penalized in the loss with a fixed weight, as shown in Fig. 1. In our view, this has three major drawbacks: (i) Unrolled repair is compute- and memory-intensive, which limits applicability to relatively cheap-to-evaluate constraints (e.g., closed-form expressions). (ii) Current methods softly penalize post-repair constraint residuals. When the projection iterates do not converge (e.g., under extremely non-linear constraints), a soft penalty does not provide enough training pressure to satisfy these constraints. (iii) We believe that the repair should only act as a final clean-up, and the neural net should learn the bulk of constraint satisfaction. Methods that train with unrolled repair tend to transfer the majority constraint satisfaction work to the repair step. To address these issues, our contributions are as follows: 1. Single-Step Repair in Training. We show that, contrary to the prevailing wisdom, only a 

single detached projection step suffices in training while matching or outperforming multi-step unrolled-repair baselines (§5.1—§5.3). This avoids backpropagation through the repair, and enables tractability in applications where unrolled-repair training is infeasible (§5.2).

# 3 Related Work 

We focus on settings where Φ is expensive and lacks any exploitable algebraic form, but is smoothly differentiable. Approaches enforcing constraints by construction via hand-specified output lay-ers (Beucler et al., 2021) or differentiable optimization layers (Amos & Kolter, 2017; Agrawal et al., 2019) usually require known structure (linearity, convexity, . . . ) that Φ does not expose, and are thus out of scope here. 

Penalty Methods. A straightforward approach is to incorporate constraints directly into the training loss: Lc ∼ wc · c(ˆ y), with penalty multipliers wc either fixed or propagated adaptively (Platt & Barr, 1988; Basir & Senocak, 2023). However, these soft methods often fail to satisfy constraints exactly (Donti et al., 2021). A naive solution is to “bolt on” a repair procedure at test-time. However, this can result in poor solution quality after projection because the trained model fails to anticipate the repair operation (Chu et al., 2026; Grontas et al., 2026), a finding we corroborate in our own experiments. Recent approaches therefore use the repair procedure in end-to-end training. We now discuss prominent variants of this idea. 

Repair Methods. Projection-based methods evaluate constraint residuals c and their Jacobian J

to perform a linearized quasi-orthogonal projection from (˜ y(i)) onto the feasible set M. Because the linearization introduces higher-order error, multiple projection steps are typically used at both training and inference time, often with auto-stopping once feasibility is reached. Recent examples are HardNet (Min & Azizan, 2024) (restricted to linear constraints), Πnet (Grontas et al., 2026) (restricted to analytically known convex constraints), ENFORCE (Lastrucci & Schweidtmann, 2025), or SnareNet (Chu et al., 2026), which uses Levenberg–Marquardt (LM) projection, improving projection stability when the constraint Jacobian is near-degenerate. We use a similar projector (see Eq. 8), albeit with adaptive damping according to Yamashita & Fukushima (2001). To solve the issue of slow convergence in early training, some methods use adaptive constraint relaxation in early training (Chu et al., 2026), or warm-up using plain penalty methods (Lastrucci & Schweidtmann, 2025). DC3 (Donti et al., 2021) decomposes optimization variables into independent ( ˆyindep ) and dependent ( ydep ) subsets, predicting the former via the neural backbone and recovering the latter via projection to satisfy equality constraints, with inequalities handled via gradient descent. DC3 requires that equality constraints have known dependence on ydep . Instead of projection, FSNet (Nguyen & Donti, 2025) employs gradient descent to minimize ∥h(y(i))∥2 + ∥g(y(i))∥2 at repair step i.FSNet carries formal convergence guarantees, and unlike projection methods, avoids full Jacobian construction, which scales favorably with constraint count. Its core limitation is iteration count. It requires more repair steps N than iterative projection methods, making backpropagation memory-intensive. L-BFGS (Liu & Nocedal, 1989) with early stopping and truncated unrolled differentiation mitigate but do not resolve this. 

Gap. Existing methods (DC3, SnareNet, ENFORCE, FSNet, . . . ) as shown under SOTA in Fig. 1 share three limitations. First, they unroll multiple repair steps per training iteration and backpropagate through them, which is intractable for very expensive evaluators Φ. Second, they train on post-repair constraint residuals using soft penalties with fixed scalar weights wc, w d:

L = f (˜ y) + wd∥ˆy − ˜y(N )∥2 + wc∥c(˜ y(N ))∥2.

When the unrolled projector drives c(˜ y(N )) ≈ 0 during training, the constraint loss term ( wc) exerts no pressure on the backbone and the repair carries all of the feasibility work as is shown later in Fig. 2. When the unroll does not converge, on the other hand, the residual that remains competes with f (˜ y) through the fixed weight wc, and feasibility degrades as we show in §5.3.

## 4.1 PAL Training 

Algorithm 1 summarizes PAL training. We now discuss the three subroutines P ROJECT , L OSS (Eq. 1), and L OG GAP UPDATE (Eq. 2). 

Projection. Our core insight is that, in training, it is sufficient to apply a single repair step instead of a full unroll. Given a prediction ˆy ∼ Nθ, we perform a single forward pass on the evaluator, 

Φ(ˆ y) 7 → c(ˆ y), f (ˆ y), and K backward passes to assemble the Jacobian J. We then perform a single projection iterate. In our experiments, we use Levenberg–Marquardt with damping according to Yamashita & Fukushima (2001) as the default. PAL is also compatible with other projectors (e.g., Sequential Quadratic Programming (SQP) or Interior-Point (IP) as described in Appendix D). Note that at test time , we use up to 10 projection steps with early stopping, just as our baselines do.

Baselines. We compare against ENFORCE (Lastrucci & Schweidtmann, 2025), FSNet (Nguyen & Donti, 2025), SnareNet (Chu et al., 2026), and DC3 (Donti et al., 2021)where applicable. We also include ALM (Basir & Senocak, 2023) (an adaptive penalty method) with Bolt-On repair, i.e., the repair step is only used at test time. The ALM+Bolt-On baseline uses the same LM repair step with adaptive damping as PAL. All other methods use their own repair methods (DC3 uses undamped completion, FSNet uses gradient descent, SnareNet uses damped LM with fixed damping, etc.). 

Metrics. All experiments report the Objective as f (˜ y(N )) and Feasibility (%) , the fraction of evaluation samples whose final output ˜y(N ) satisfies all constraints to within the benchmark-specific tolerance (typically 10 −3 or 10 −4). To compare compute, we report Evaluator forward passes N fΦ

and Evaluator backward passes N bΦ, normalized by batch size. 

## 5.1 Comparison on Synthetic Stress-Test Benchmarks 

In Table 1 we compare results on six benchmarks, each adding a specific stressor. S1 is the simplest with a single nonlinear equality constraint and a non-convex objective. S2 adds inequality constraints and a non-convex chained Rosenbrock objective. S3 features an ill-conditioned objective with constraint coupling. S4 has nonlinear coupling between two variable blocks. S5 is an overdetermined nonlinear equality system, whose feasible set reduces to a single point per condition z. S6 is high-dimensional with K = 90 constraints, including redundant inequalities. By construction, projection alone cannot reach the constrained optimum on S1 –S4 and S6 . All synthetic benchmarks have a known constrained optimum f ⋆ = 0 . More details on S1 –S6 can be found in Appendix I. All methods are hyperparameter-tuned with Bayesian optimization, which is described in further detail in Appendix M. All baselines reach high feasibility except DC3 due to its undamped completion step, which fails on ill-conditioned problems. With PAL this failure mode can be replicated when the LM projection is used without damping (see App. B). PAL is competitive with or outperforms repair-aware baselines on optimality across S1 –S6 . EN-FORCE is the best baseline, except on S5 where it is structurally inapplicable. ALM+Bolt-On reaches near-perfect feasibility but very poor optimality. Since the projector is held constant between PAL and ALM+Bolt-On, this isolates ALM’s lack of repair-awareness as the cause for ALM+Bolt-On’s poor performance, which corroborates prior observations that training without repair-awareness can be counterproductive (Chu et al., 2026; Grontas et al., 2026). SnareNet underperforms on objective across the sweep. FSNet performs well, except on S5 .Per training step, PAL requires two forward passes through Φ (at ˆy and ˜y(1) ) and K + 1 backward passes at ˆy, of which K build the Jacobian J. In practice, we cache J and reuse it to backpropagate the training loss (Eq. 1) without a second pass through Φ. PAL uses the fewest evaluator passes of the repair-aware methods for problems with few constraints (moderate K) such as S1 . At, high 

K (e.g., S6 ) FSNet, which sidesteps Jacobian assembly, requires fewer backward passes. Among projection methods, PAL requires fewest. ALM+Bolt-On is cheapest overall but achieves poor results. Appendix O elaborates further on compute scaling.

## 5.4 Ablation Studies 

In Appendix B, we ablate PAL’s loss and weight update mechanisms and their hyperparameters. Slowing the adaptive weight growth via γ or Dmax (recall Eq. 2 for definitions) can improve optimality, while an excessive growth rate γ can collapse optimality unless mitigated by low Dmax .We find that outcomes are insensitive to the threshold τd across several orders of magnitude. # 6 Conclusion 

Contributions and Outcome. We introduced PAL, a scheme where single-step projection metrics drive adaptive constraint weights. We show that with this method, fully unrolled repair is not necessary to achieve good feasibility or optimality, and a single step is in fact sufficient. In fact, PAL is the only method that achieves best-in-class results simultaneously on synthetic stress-testing (Tab. 1), engineering applications (Tab. 2), and under extremely high curvature (Fig. 3). In §5.2 we show that using only a single detached repair step enables tractable training when constraints are expensive to evaluate, like with high-fidelity physics surrogates. Current full-unroll methods were not able to train on these problems due to memory. Using only a single detached step also results in fewer constraint evaluator calls (§5.1), and PAL trains 2.5 × faster than DC3 on its flagship benchmark (while still providing better feasibility and optimality). Lastly, PAL’s adaptive weight scheme shifts feasibility towards the neural backbone (Fig. 2) which soft-loss unrolled baselines do not do. Under extremely non-linear constraints, this manifests in PAL retaining near-full feasibility while unrolled soft-loss baselines degrade (§5.3). 

Limitations. Several limitations remain: (1) Like all other projection-based methods, we require the constraint evaluation to be differentiable. (2) In all repair-based methods, feasibility is defined w.r.t. the evaluator Φ. If Φ is inaccurate, constraints will be satisfied according to an incorrect reference. (3) Assembly of the Jacobian requires K backward passes through the evaluator Φ for all projection-based methods. PAL alleviates this issue somewhat as it only requires a single projection step, but problems where the number of constraints K is extremely large are still compute-intensive. And (4), PAL introduces several hyperparameters ( γ, τd, τc, Dmax , δ, λmin ), though in practice τc

follows immediately from problem-specific tolerances, Dmax can be left at a conservative default, and δ and λmin have negligible impact. 

Future Work. A first natural extension is to study how PAL performs on tasks beyond amortized optimization, where the objective f is a data-driven loss (e.g., MSE). Second, efficiency improvements or auto-calibration of the remaining hyperparameters could make single-step repair with PAL even more easily usable in scientific and engineering applications. Examples include estimating ¯wd and 

¯wc from sparse projection only every few steps, or auto-calibrating γ and Dmax from early-trajectory metrics or τd from the relative error between f (ˆ y) and f (˜ y(1) )). Third, since the repair step is never backpropagated through, PAL does not require a differentiable projector. This opens the door to bespoke, non-differentiable repair methods that are specialized to niche applications beyond the projection methods explored here.

# E Additional Details on f (˜ y) vs. f (ˆ y) for Training Loss 

In this appendix, we hypothesize about the causes of stalled training when f (˜ y(1) ) is used in the PAL training loss. Intuitively, one would think that f (˜ y) is the logical choice, since it is the objective after projection. One would want to take the change in objective by the projection into account. This is also the motivation for using f (˜ y(N )) in the baseline SOTA methods we considered in § 3. However, we have found that using f (˜ y) directly in the loss can have disastrous consequences on training convergence of the objective when projection is enabled in early training. In short, this is because using f (˜ y) involves backpropagating its gradients through the projector. Here, we sketch out in rough terms that, because of how the projector is formulated for us (damped LM), the gradients

Implications for PAL. In practice, to circumvent this issue with PAL, there are three approaches to deal with the effect described above: 1. Pre-projection objective . This is what we report on using throughout in this paper and, while not perfect in theory, is the most straightforward solution in practice. This could be especially relevant for K ≫ 1,where assembly of the constraint Jacobian J is expensive.

Active Set. The active constraint vector cA collects all violated constraints c(y) > 0. JA(y) := 

∂c A/∂y is the Jacobian of the active constraint vector. The local feasible manifold of the active constraints is MA := {y : cA(y) = 0 }.The LM step treats all active constraints as equalities and does not consider the inactive inequalities, i.e., they are deleted from the linear system. A mask is used for this using c(˜ y(i)) before every iterate. In effect, an inequality that a step overshoots enters the active set at the next iterate, and one that becomes satisfied leaves it. The active set can therefore switch between iterates at inference. In practice, we did not experience any issues with active-set switching. The convergence statement below assumes a fixed active set. The two alternative repair steps (SQP and Interior-Point) in Appendix D do not use this masking, since they both have native ways to account for non-violated inequalities (quadratic subproblem for SQP, barrier weight for IP).

## F.1 Convergence 

We fix a neighborhood U ⊂ Rd on which the active set A is constant, and let cA : U → RKA denote the corresponding smooth active residual map, with Jacobian JA(y) := ∇cA(y). The local active feasible set is MA := {y ∈ U : cA(y) = 0 }.

Assumption 1 (Local active-set regularity) . There exists a point y⋆ ∈ M A and a neighborhood U of 

y⋆ such that: 1. the active set A is fixed on U .2. cA is continuously differentiable on U , and JA is Lipschitz continuous on U .3. cA satisfies a local error bound on U : there exists κ > 0 such that

# J Chip Placement Case Study 

Here, we show one additional application where the constraint and objective evaluation is relatively cheap, so that methods with unrolled repair in training are memory-feasible. B = 30 rectangular blocks on a 200 ×200 canvas to minimise a wirelength proxy (a weighted sum of pairwise Manhattan distances between connected block centres and boundary pins) plus a soft bounding-box area penalty (LSE-smoothed). A single equality constraint requires the total overlap volume across all B(B−1) /2 block pairs to be zero. The design vector y ∈ R3B (3B = 90 variables) encodes block centres (xi, y i) and raw widths si. Widths decode as wi = softplus( si) + 0 .5, and heights follow from a fixed per-instance target area, keeping all boxes non-degenerate and area-preserving. We want to point out that this benchmark is a lightweight component placement task and a strongly simplified FloorSet derivative. We did not investigate in further detail why this is the case, but believe both FSNet and SnareNet could perform better on this task if their hyperparameters were tuned extensively. However, orthogonal to this, we want to point out that PAL 23 Preprint is significantly faster to train and achieves 100% feasibility despite not being tuned for the problem at hand. Computationally, this benchmark is very well suited to PAL since it only has a single constraint (K = 1 ), meaning that the constraint Jacobian J is cheap to build. ALM+Bolt-On reaches 100% feasibility on 8 of 10 seeds, but the remaining 2 seeds carry very high constraint violations, which is what drives the mean to 80% ±42% in Fig. 6. This experiment was run in parallel (across seeds and methods) on a 288-core ARM CPU node. Cross-method ratios for wall-clock time are reliable, but absolute wall-clock numbers are hardware- and contention-specific.

K.1.4 Iso-slicing 

In order to couple the surface pressure model (FiLM BlendedNet) and our structural model, we employ a deterministic geometry extraction algorithm that queries the neural SDF using a quadtree-based marching-squares level-set extraction to find a discrete set of surface query points rs with outward normals n and arc-length integration weights, suitable for chordwise quadrature of the FiLM predictions. At each spanwise station yi, the BWB geometry is sliced with this iso-contour prim-itive prog.isocontour(plane=’xz’, stations=y_unit, normal_mode=’3d’) ,which returns 2D polylines on the chordwise plane plus matching 3D outward surface normals and per-segment arc-length weights. Default quadtree settings use base resolution 8 with 5 levels of refinement, so the SDF is queried repeatedly per slice. After extraction, each chordwise polyline 

(x, z ) is lifted to 3D by inserting the unit-frame spanwise station value as y. The station coordinate is detached from the gradient graph since it is a sampling location, not a design variable, such that the gradient graph only routes to xskin , L, α cr . At queried stations beyond the local span the iso-contour returns no polyline. The corresponding station’s load contributions are zeroed and stay differentiable. Downstream, the outputs of the iso-slicing procedure (rs, n, w ) are fed into the surface pressure model Φθ 

> cp

to determine cp, which is then integrated chordwise with the arc-length weights into per-station aerodynamic loads. These are shear load, moment, and center of pressure (qz , m, x cp ).

Aggregating the 45 pairwise overlaps into a single mean-pooled scalar weakened training across methods, which we believe is due to gradient cancel-lation : a building wedged between violating neighbours on opposite sides receives near-opposite push vectors that partially cancel under mean aggregation, so the gradient with respect to its center

# L Failure Modes of Baselines in Engineering Benchmarks 

This appendix collects some of our hypotheses for the failure modes observed on E1 –E3 for the baselines. We do not study each failure in depth. 

DC3 on E1 and E2 . DC3 is structurally inapplicable here. Its completion step solves the equality constraints for a dependent subset of the outputs and requires a known dependence on that subset. The single equality constraint of E1 and E2 is a neural surrogate of the full design vector, so no such subset exists, and DC3 would reduce to a non-adaptive penalty loss followed by gradient-correction steps. We therefore do not run it on E1 and E2 .

IPOPT on E2 . No IPOPT restart converges on E2 . The best iterate of every restart is feasible, but only 31% of final iterates are, and all restarts terminate with a local-infeasibility status. The aggregated constraints of E2 apply ReLU transforms, so the Jacobian rows of inactive constraints vanish identically and the constraint qualification fails at most iterates ( 95 .6% of Jacobian rows are zero at the final iterates, and the remaining rows span roughly 35 orders of magnitude). We speculate that this is the pathology Yang et al. (2022) report for IPOPT on embedded ReLU networks, where dual infeasibility is never driven to zero and the solver terminates at spurious points. On E1 , IPOPT reaches feasible points in every restart but also never exits with a converged status. 

SnareNet on E3 . On E3 , the number of constraints K = 576 is much larger than dim( y) = 128 .We believe that SnareNet was conceived around K ≤ dim( y), since its convergence proofs require a constraint Jacobian of full row rank (Chu et al., 2026). In Appendix G of the original SnareNet 31 Preprint paper, Chu et al. (2026) show experiments where SnareNet is trained under K > dim( y). They show that, on synthetic non-convex programs with K = 500 and dim( y) = 100 , SnareNet exhausts its projector budget of 100 steps without converging. However, we have no intuition for why this occurs, as Chu et al. (2026) do not elaborate on the suspected reasons for this failure beyond noting that SnareNet suits problems with K ≪ dim( y). On E3 , each repair step reduces the relaxed residual by roughly 2% , so we cap the repair budget at 5 steps, and all ten seeds diverge to non-finite losses within the first 300 epochs. 

ENFORCE on E3 . ENFORCE provides an optional gate during training. This gate rejects any projection step in training if it does not lower the soft loss. We find that, without the gate, training diverges catastrophically on E3 , with all ten seeds producing a non-finite loss in the first epoch. The gate is therefore needed to stabilize training. With the gate enabled, however, we observe that all projection steps on E3 are rejected. Effectively, ENFORCE then trains without any projection awareness. At test time, the projection is applied regardless, and we then observe divergence, resulting in numerical errors on 2 to 13 of the 64 test queries per seed. 

FSNet on E3 . FSNet seeks feasibility by minimizing the summed constraint violation with L-BFGS. We speculate that this is much weaker than projection on E3 because of the extremely high number of constraints. Amalgamating them into a single score discards the per-constraint information that a full Jacobian retains. Additionally, the single score assumes somewhat comparable scales across constraints, but ACOPF constraints (voltages, thermal limits, . . . ) have mixed units and potentially very different scales. The FSNet paper itself does not use L-BFGS on its ACOPF benchmark but switches to LM for the feasibility-seeking step (Nguyen & Donti, 2025, Appendix B), which requires the constraint Jacobian in every step. This variant is not part of the public code, so our port runs the generic L-BFGS solver on E3 . We read the authors’ switch as confirmation that first-order feasibility seeking is not viable on E3 .

# O Compute Scaling 

In this appendix, we provide some thoughts on how PAL performs on problems with a large number of constraints, and how complexity scales with problem dimensionality, the number of constraints, and constraint cost heterogeneity. Table 12 shows the full results table of the synthetic benchmarking results, including full N ϕb and N ϕf , which Table 1 showed only excerpts of. 

Cost Model. Per training step, PAL costs 2Cf + ( K + 1) Cb evaluator passes, where Cf and Cb

are the costs of one forward and one backward pass (VJP) through the evaluator Φ, respectively. This is because two forward passes are needed, one before the projection at ˆy and one after the projection at ˜y(1) . K VJP backward passes are needed to build the Jacobian J, and one backward pass is needed to backpropagate the training loss (weighted constraints and the objective). For the special case of K ≫ dim( y), J can be assembled from forward-mode JVPs instead of reverse-mode VJPs, meaning the assembly cost is min( K, dim( y)) passes. Additionally, PAL requires O(K2 dim( y) + K3) for the damped normal system of the LM projector. In the regime we target, the evaluator passes dominate and the latter is negligible. The recorded number of constraint evaluator calls in Table 1 fits this model exactly (e.g., 91 backward calls at K = 90 on S6 ). 

Comparison with Full-Unroll Projection Methods. Every projection-based method (DC3, EN-FORCE, SnareNet) assembles the same K-row Jacobian, but unrolled methods do so at each of N

repair iterates. This results in an overall cost of N (Cf + KC b) versus PAL’s single assembly. Comparison with FSNet. FSNet sidesteps Jacobian assembly and requires only N (Cf + Cb), but unrolls many more repair steps (large N ). The break-even point between PAL and FSNet is roughly at K ≈ 2N . FSNet must still store the unrolled tape, which can be memory-intractable (see Table 2). 

Heterogeneous Constraint Complexity. In some applications, one or more constraints might be extremely expensive to compute and dominate compute cost. For example, E2 ’s wind-speed danger constraint shown in Appendix K.2 is evaluated using diffusion model unrolls, while other constraints in this problem are trivial to compute (e.g., box-to-box overlaps, analytical closed-form constraints, etc.). In this situation, the Jacobian assembly count K virtually drops out of PAL’s cost while FSNet still pays the full unroll. Other than cost, we do not see any specific reasons PAL might struggle with large-K. Benchmark 

E3 in Table 2 illustrates that PAL can be applied without any modifications to applications with a very high number of constraints ( K = 576 ) and trains faster than DC3 thanks to single-step training, despite building the full Jacobian (which DC3 does not, as it only projects equalities). 36
