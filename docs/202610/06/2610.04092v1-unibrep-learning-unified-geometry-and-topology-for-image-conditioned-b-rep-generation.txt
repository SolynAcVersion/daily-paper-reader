Abstract 

Generating a boundary representation (B-rep) conditioned on a single image requires faithful reconstruction of geometry, valid topology, and support for complex shapes. We present UniBRep, a geometry-first framework that adapts a pretrained image-to-3D model to generate a feature mesh as a unified intermediate representation. Its surface provides a geometric scaffold, while spatially aligned learned features encode face-separation cues for topology recovery. Dual decoder branches generate the geometry and face-separation features; a geometry- and feature-guided construction pipeline then fits parametric surfaces, recovers boundary curves and connectivity, and assembles an explicit B-rep using a CAD kernel. Recovering topology from mesh regions avoids predefined architectural face-count limits, allowing face count to scale with shape complexity. On the standard DeepCAD benchmark, UniBRep produces valid B-reps for 80.49% of inputs and reduces face Chamfer distance from 0.1096 to 0.0345 relative to CADDreamer. In a matched comparison, UniBRep also outperforms the HoLa public demo across all reported metrics. Further evaluations demonstrate scalability to high-complexity shapes beyond the standard 30-face range, generalization to objects outside the CAD training distribution, and qualitative transfer to real photographs.

1. Introduction 

Boundary representations (B-reps) are widely used for 3D model-ing in computer-aided design (CAD), with applications spanning manufacturing [FPPM14, JM97], architecture [CS13, Liv08], prod-uct design [Cha14, Tov89], and digital content creation. Unlike implicit fields [MST ∗22, WLL ∗21] or polygonal meshes, B-reps encode object boundaries using parametric surfaces and boundary curves, together with topology specifying the connectivity among faces, edges, and vertices. This explicit representation enables pre-arXiv:2610.04092v1 [cs.CV] 2 Oct 2026 2 H. Ying et al. / UniBRep: Learning Unified Geometry and Topology for Image-conditioned B-Rep Generation 

cise editing, simulation, and fabrication. Single-image B-rep gen-eration has recently gained attention in the 3D vision and graph-ics community. While conventional 3D generation primarily targets visually plausible geometry, B-rep generation must also produce structurally valid CAD models suitable for downstream use. Reli-able single-image B-rep generation could support reverse engineer-ing, design retrieval, rapid prototyping, and image-based modeling, although recovering complete geometry and topology from a single image remains highly challenging. This difficulty arises from three coupled requirements: faith-ful geometry, valid topology, and support for shapes of varying complexity. Direct B-rep generation methods [XLJ ∗24, LZJK25, LXY ∗25] jointly predict faces, edges, vertices, and their topolog-ical relationships. Local errors in these predictions can propagate through B-rep assembly, causing gaps, inconsistent adjacency, non-watertight surfaces, or invalid CAD models. Many such methods also generate fixed-size or bounded sets of B-rep entities, imposing predefined face-count limits that restrict scalability. Geometry-first methods mitigate these limitations by generat-ing surface geometry before recovering B-rep structure. CAD-Dreamer [LLL ∗25] follows this approach by predicting multi-view normal and primitive maps from a single image, reconstructing a mesh, and converting it into a B-rep through a multi-stage post-processing pipeline. Although this formulation avoids a predefined face-count limit, its final quality depends on both the fidelity of the generated geometry and the robustness of the subsequent B-rep construction, where errors may accumulate across stages. More-over, geometry generators trained only on CAD data may lack the broad 3D priors needed to generalize across diverse inputs. We propose UniBRep , a geometry-first framework for single-image-conditioned B-rep generation. UniBRep adapts TREL-LIS [XLX ∗25], a pretrained image-to-3D foundation model, to generate a feature mesh as a unified intermediate representation: its surface provides a geometric scaffold, while its learned features en-code face-separation cues for topology recovery. A reconstruction pipeline converts this representation into an explicit B-rep by fitting parametric surfaces and recovering boundary curves and connectiv-ity. By recovering topology from generated mesh regions, UniBRep largely preserves the generated geometry while supporting a vari-able number of faces without a predefined architectural limit. Ex-periments demonstrate improved geometric and topological qual-ity, B-rep validity, and geometry preservation over prior methods. Further evaluations show that UniBRep scales to high-complexity shapes, generalizes to objects outside the CAD training distribu-tion, and can be applied to real-world images. In summary, our contributions are: 

• A geometry-first framework that adapts a pretrained image-to-3D prior to generate feature meshes as a unified representation for B-rep geometry and topology recovery. 

• A B-rep reconstruction pipeline that converts generated feature meshes into high-quality CAD models while allowing face count to scale with shape complexity. 

• Strong B-rep generation performance, scalability to high-complexity shapes, and generalization to out-of-distribution ob-jects and real-world images. 

2. Related Work 

Recent CAD generation methods broadly fall into three categories: construction-program generation, direct B-rep generation, and ge-ometry generation followed by CAD construction. Program-based approaches represent CAD models as sequences of parametric op-erations, such as sketching, extrusion, and Boolean operations. DeepCAD [WXZ21] introduces a transformer-based framework for CAD program generation, while subsequent work improves controllability and extends this paradigm to image-conditioned generation [XWL ∗22, CWC ∗25]. Although construction programs are interpretable and editable, these methods typically require se-quence annotations and predefined operation vocabularies. These constraints limit their ability to represent shapes involving unsup-ported operations or surface types, such as fillets, sweeps, cones, spheres, and free-form surfaces. The second category directly models B-rep geometry and topol-ogy through tokens, sequences, graphs, complexes, or latent repre-sentations. HoLa [LXY ∗25] uses a holistic surface latent for con-ditional B-rep generation on the filtered DeepCAD benchmark. Other methods generate B-rep surfaces and topological entities using chain complexes, diffusion models, or autoregressive mod-els [GLP ∗22,XLJ ∗24,LZJK25,LFC25,GHJ ∗25,XJL ∗25,LZQ ∗25, JLD ∗23]. However, directly generating structured B-rep entities or fixed-size surface representations can make assembly sensitive to local errors, leading to gaps, inconsistent connectivity, or invalid B-reps. Several methods also impose predefined limits on the num-ber of faces or topological entities, restricting their ability to scale with shape complexity. UniBRep instead recovers topology from generated mesh regions, allowing face count to scale with shape complexity without a predefined architectural limit. Finally, geometry-first methods construct structured CAD rep-resentations from intermediate geometry. CADDreamer [LLL ∗25] predicts multi-view normal and primitive maps from a single im-age, generates a mesh, and constructs a B-rep from optimized an-alytic primitive intersections. Point2CAD [LOWS24] and NVD-Net [LCP ∗24] recover parametric primitives from point clouds. Although these methods avoid direct B-rep entity generation, er-rors in the geometric scaffold can propagate through subsequent B-rep construction. UniBRep instead adapts TRELLIS [XLX ∗25], whose large-scale image-to-3D pretraining provides a broad geom-etry prior that supports generalization beyond the CAD training distribution. It uses TRELLIS’s structured latent representation and FlexiCubes-based decoding [SMH ∗23] to generate feature meshes that combine surface geometry with learned face-separation cues. A B-rep construction pipeline then fits analytic and free-form sur-faces and recovers topology from the feature mesh while largely preserving the generated geometry.

3.1. Feature Mesh Representation 

UniBRep represents the intermediate output of image-conditioned generation as a feature mesh G = ( M, F), where M =(VM, EM, TM) is a triangular surface mesh and F is a feature field aligned with its surface. For each surface point x ∈ M , the feature 

f(x) ∈ Rd f is a learned descriptor of its B-rep face membership. The mesh captures the generated geometry, while the feature field provides cues for recovering its face partition. The feature field is learned as an instance embedding rather than a fixed semantic labeling. Features associated with points on the same B-rep face are encouraged to be similar, whereas features be-longing to different faces are separated. Consequently, the surface features can be clustered into a variable number of candidate face regions without requiring predefined face labels or an upper bound on the number of faces. The feature mesh does not directly encode the complete B-rep topology. Instead, it provides a shared geometric domain that spa-tially aligns face-partition cues with the generated surface. Bound-ary curves, vertices, and their connectivity are subsequently recov-ered from the partitioned mesh. 

3.2. Image-to-Feature-Mesh Generation 

We build on TRELLIS [XLX ∗25], a pretrained image-to-3D foun-dation model based on Structured Latent Representations (SLat). Given an input image I, the sparse-structure (SS) generator GSS 

first predicts a set of active voxels V, then the SLat generator GSLat 

predicts a latent feature zi ∈ Rd for each active voxel pi:

V = GSS (I), zV = GSLat (I, V) = {(pi, zi)}pi∈V . (1) The active voxels describe the coarse spatial structure of the object, while their latent features encode local geometric information. The SS and SLat generators use image-conditioned transformer back-bones, while the SLat decoder DSLat progressively upsamples the sparse features into dense output fields. We adapt the SS and SLat generators to the CAD domain by in-serting low-rank adaptation (LoRA) modules [HSW ∗22] into their transformer blocks. Following TRELLIS’s training formulation, we optimize both generators using conditional flow-matching objec-tives on structured latent targets constructed from tessellated CAD models. TRELLIS’s large-scale pretraining on 3D assets provides a broad geometric prior that supports generalization beyond the CAD shapes used for adaptation.

Here, γ contains the geometric quantities required by Flex-iCubes [SMH ∗23], while H is a dense grid of eight-dimensional face-separation features. 3.3.1. Geometry-Guided Segmentation and Surface Fitting 

We first partition M into connected candidate face regions us-ing region growing [AB94] based on mesh adjacency, boundary strength, and normal consistency. Because this procedure operates directly on the mesh, it produces a variable number of regions with-out assuming a predefined face count. For each region, we sequentially fit planes, cylinders, cones, spheres, and tori using RANSAC [SWK07]. A primitive is accepted only if it meets the prescribed thresholds for support ratio and ge-ometric residual. Regions that cannot be adequately represented by any supported primitive are passed to feature-guided refinement. 

3.3.2. Feature-Guided Refinement and Free-Form Fitting 

For each region that fails initial primitive fitting, we cluster its learned per-vertex features using mean shift. Because the feature embedding is trained to group samples from the same B-rep face and separate samples from different faces, the resulting clusters re-veal subdivisions that may not be apparent from local geometry. A feature cluster may contain spatially disconnected parts of the mesh. We therefore divide each cluster into connected components based on mesh adjacency, treat each component as a candidate re-gion, and repeat analytic primitive fitting using the same criteria. Regions successfully fitted during the initial geometry-based stage remain unchanged, so feature guidance is introduced only where geometric fitting is insufficient. A refined region that still cannot be represented by a sup-ported analytic primitive is parameterized using FlattenAny-thing [ZHWH24] and fitted with a B-spline surface. This fallback enables the reconstruction of irregular and free-form regions with-out forcing them into the predefined analytic surface families. 

3.3.3. Boundary Recovery and B-Rep Assembly 

After assigning a fitted surface to every face region, we recover B-rep boundaries directly from the segmented mesh. For each pair of adjacent regions, we trace their shared mesh boundary to ob-tain a candidate edge polyline. Unlike pipelines based on explicit surface–surface intersection [LOWS24], this procedure preserves the adjacency of the generated mesh and is less sensitive to small discrepancies between independently fitted surfaces. Following BrepGen [XLJ ∗24], we approximate each boundary polyline with a cubic B-spline curve and resample it along its arc length. We fix endpoints shared by multiple curves to common vertex positions to maintain consistency among neighboring trim-ming loops. The resulting incidences among face regions, boundary curves, and shared endpoints determine the face–edge and edge– vertex connectivity. For every face incident to a recovered 3D boundary curve, we project the curve onto its fitted surface to obtain a parametric curve that defines the trimming boundary. Using the recovered topology, we assemble the trimmed faces, shared curves, and vertices into a B-rep and then sew and heal it with OpenCascade. The resulting B-rep explicitly represents faces, edges, vertices, surfaces, curves, and their incidence relations, enabling standard CAD-kernel operations. Because topology is recovered from a mesh partition rather than generated as a bounded collection of en-tities, UniBRep does not impose a predefined architectural upper bound on the number of faces. Moreover, local surface fitting and direct boundary tracing largely preserve the geometry of the gen-erated feature mesh. We evaluate this preservation in Sec. 5.1. 4. Experiments 

We train UniBRep by finetuning TRELLIS [XLX ∗25] on Deep-CAD [WXZ21], a filtered subset of ABC [KMJ ∗19]. We use BRep-Gen’s [XLJ ∗24] data split and follow HoLa [LXY ∗25] by exclud-ing models with fewer than seven B-rep faces, yielding 64,049 training, 4,466 validation, and 2,605 test models. Each source B-rep is tessellated with OpenCascade and normalized to [−0.5, 0.5]3.For each training model, we render 150 random views for SLat tar-gets and 25 random views for image conditioning; inference uses a single RGB image. The SS and SLat generators use rank-32 LoRA modules, while the geometry and feature decoder branches use rank 16. Appendix A provides additional implementation details. 

4.1. Baselines 

We compare with CADDreamer [LLL ∗25], a geometry-first image-conditioned CAD generation method, and HoLa [LXY ∗25], a di-rect conditional B-rep generator. For each comparison, we use the same underlying test object and viewing direction but render the input required by each pipeline. UniBRep receives an RGB image, while CADDreamer receives an orthographically rendered ground-truth normal map. HoLa does not release the model weights, infer-ence code, or evaluation code needed to reproduce its published re-sults, so we evaluate its public Hugging Face demo using an RGB rendering from the same viewing direction with black edge over-lays matching the input style shown in its paper. We evaluate the HoLa demo and UniBRep outputs using the common protocol de-scribed in Sec. 4.3; Appendix B compares the demo results with HoLa’s published results and analyzes the protocol discrepancy. HoLa’s released configuration supports at most 30 faces, whereas UniBRep has no predefined architectural face-count cap. We additionally evaluate point-cloud-based reconstruction meth-ods NVDNet [LCP ∗24] and Point2CAD [LOWS24] as controlled baselines using UniBRep intermediate outputs. NVDNet receives points sampled from our generated geometry, while Point2CAD re-ceives our generated geometry and segmentation. Both output para-metric surfaces rather than B-reps, so valid-output ratios are not re-ported. These comparisons isolate the contributions of our learned face-separation features and B-rep construction strategy.

4.3. Metrics 

Following NVDNet [LCP ∗24], we match generated and ground-truth B-rep entities using Hungarian matching and compute Cham-fer distance and geometry F-score for vertices, edges, and faces. We also report topology F-score for face–edge (FE) and edge–vertex (EV) connectivity, valid-output ratio, and mesh-segmentation F-score. To assess geometry preservation, we compare the Chamfer distances of generated meshes and final B-reps to ground truth. Fol-lowing BrepDiff [LZJK25], a valid output must be a readable sin-gle OpenCascade solid that passes wire and edge checks and can be tessellated into a nonempty mesh; detailed checks are provided in Appendix B. For methods that generate B-reps, geometry and topology metrics are averaged over valid outputs. To support reproducibility, we will publicly release our code, model weights, data splits, and generated outputs.

5.2. High-Complexity and Out-of-Distribution Inputs 

Table 4 evaluates UniBRep beyond the standard DeepCAD bench-mark along two axes: shape complexity and distribution shift. Although validity is lower than on the standard 7–30-face bench-mark, UniBRep retains a substantial advantage over the baselines on these more complex shapes. Fig. 6 plots performance by face count. UniBRep achieves the highest surface and face–edge topology F-scores in every bin and retains its topology advantage beyond 30 faces. Fig. 7 further shows recovery of holes, repeated thin parts, and detailed edge layouts on DeepCAD objects with 31 or more faces. 6. Limitations and Future Work 

Single-image B-rep generation is inherently ambiguous because occluded geometry, fine CAD details, and exact topology cannot al-ways be inferred from one view. Final quality depends on the gen-erated mesh and subsequent segmentation, surface fitting, topol-ogy extraction, and CAD-kernel assembly; errors can accumulate across these stages. On the 2,424-object DeepCAD benchmark, 0.5% fail topology extraction, 4 .0% fail B-rep assembly, and 14 .9% produce outputs that fail strict validity checks; 80 .5% fail strict post-construction validity checks. Thus, most failures occur after topology extraction. Fig. 10 illustrates failures in geometry gener-ation, thin-structure segmentation, and B-rep assembly. Although UniBRep has no predefined architectural face-count limit, valid-ity falls to 37 .57% on DeepCAD 31+ Faces, showing that highly complex topology remains challenging. It nevertheless outperforms CADDreamer and the HoLa demo across all corresponding evalua-tions. Quantitative evaluation remains concentrated on DeepCAD; performance on broader free-form and NURBS-heavy datasets re-mains untested. The resulting B-reps support standard CAD opera-tions but do not recover design history or parametric constraints. Future work could incorporate richer conditioning modalities, such as multi-view images or point clouds, and expand training and evaluation to broader free-form CAD datasets. More tightly integrating topology-aware feature learning with B-rep construc-tion could reduce heuristic refinement and improve CAD-kernel as-sembly robustness. UniBRep could also serve as an image-to-B-rep module within agentic or programmatic CAD workflows, providing Image Object B-Rep

7. Conclusion 

We presented UniBRep, a geometry-first framework that adapts TRELLIS to generate feature meshes combining surface geome-try with spatially aligned face-separation cues. A geometry- and feature-guided construction pipeline fits parametric surfaces, traces boundaries, and recovers explicit B-rep topology without a prede-fined architectural face-count limit. Experiments demonstrate sub-stantial improvements in geometric and topological quality and output validity over the evaluated baselines, together with gen-eration beyond the standard 30-face range and transfer to out-of-distribution objects and real photographs. Controlled analyses confirm the benefits of learned face features and mesh-boundary-based construction while showing that construction largely pre-serves the generated geometry. Appendix A: Additional Implementation Details 

Data and Target Construction 

We construct CAD supervision targets from DeepCAD [WXZ21], a filtered subset of ABC [KMJ ∗19]. Each source B-rep is normalized to [−0.5, 0.5]3 and tessellated with OpenCascade while preserving the correspondence between mesh triangles and their source B-rep faces. Following TRELLIS [XLX ∗25], we obtain sparse-structure targets by voxelizing the tessellated meshes at 64 3 resolution. For SLat targets, we encode multiview images into DINO features, back-project these features onto the active voxels, and pass the re-sulting sparse feature volume through the pretrained TRELLIS en-coder. We randomly render 150 views per model at 512 × 512 resolu-tion for SLat encoding and decoder supervision, along with 25 ad-ditional random views for image conditioning. Camera viewpoints are sampled on a sphere of radius 2.0 using a Hammersley sequence with a random offset, with each camera oriented toward the object center. Rendering uses diffuse Lambertian shading implemented in Open3D, and one conditioning view is randomly selected for each training instance. For face-level supervision, each sampled ground-truth surface point inherits its source B-rep face identifier. We transfer these la-bels to sampled predicted-mesh vertices through nearest-neighbor search and enforce a minimum number of samples per face so that small faces remain represented during feature learning.

The SLat representation has a sparse resolution of 64 3, with inactive voxels omitted, and the decoder progressively refines and upsamples it to predict dense geometry and feature attributes at 256 3 resolution. Boundary recovery and B-rep assembly. After assigning a fit-ted surface to each face region, we trace shared mesh bound-aries between adjacent regions to obtain candidate edge polylines without explicitly intersecting the fitted surfaces. Following Brep-Gen [XLJ ∗24], we approximate each polyline with a cubic B-spline curve and uniformly resample it at 32 locations along its arc length. Endpoints shared by multiple curves are fixed to common vertex positions. The resulting incidences among face regions, boundary curves, and shared endpoints determine the face–edge and edge– vertex connectivity. For each incident face, we project the recovered 3D boundary curve onto its fitted surface and construct a p-curve using Shape-Fix_Edge::FixAddPCurve . These p-curves define the trim-ming loops on the fitted surfaces. We assemble and sew the trimmed faces, shared curves, vertices, and recovered incidence relations using BRepBuilderAPI_Sewing . Loop consistency, face and wire orientation, and small geometric discrepancies are handled through tolerance-based healing with ShapeFix_Wire 

and ShapeFix_Face , using a precision of 0 .01 and a maximum tolerance of 0 .1. 

Appendix B: Additional Evaluation Details 

B-Rep Validation Protocol 

Following BrepDiff [LZJK25], we classify an assembled out-put as valid only if it passes all CAD-kernel and tessella-tion checks. The STEP file must be readable, contain a sin-gle top-level TopAbs_SOLID , and be reported as valid by 

BRepCheck_Analyzer . The output must also pass the wire-and edge-consistency checks adopted from BrepDiff and tessellate into a nonempty STL mesh within a 20-second timeout. Outputs that fail any check or stall during tessellation are treated as invalid.
