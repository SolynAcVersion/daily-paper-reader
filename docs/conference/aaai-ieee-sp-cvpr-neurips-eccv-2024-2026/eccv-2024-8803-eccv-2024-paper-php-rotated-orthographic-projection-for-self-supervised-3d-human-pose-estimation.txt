Abstract. Reprojection consistency is widely used for self-supervised 3D human pose estimation. However, few efforts have been made to ad-dress the inherent limitations of reprojection consistency. Lacking camera parameters and absolute position, self-supervised methods map 3D poses to 2D using orthographic projection, whereas 2D inputs are derived from perspective projection. This discrepancy among the projection models creates an offset between the supervision and prediction spaces, limiting the performance potential. To address this problem, we propose rotated orthographic projection, which achieves a geometric approximation of the perspective projection by adding a rotation operation before the or-thographic projection. Further, we optimize the reference point selection according to the human body structure and propose group rotated ortho-graphic projection, which significantly narrows the gap between the two projection models. Meanwhile, the reprojection consistency loss fails to constrain the Z-axis reverse wrong pose in 3D space. Therefore, we intro-duce the joint reverse constraint to limit the range of angles between the local reference plane and the end joints, penalizing unrealistic 3D poses and clarifying the Z-axis orientation of the model. The proposed method achieves state-of-the-art (SOTA) performance on both Human3.6M and MPII-INF-3DHP datasets. Particularly, it reduces the mean error from 65.9mm to 42.9mm (34.9% improvement) over the SOTA self-supervised method on Human3.6M. 

Keywords: self-supervised · 3D human pose estimation · reprojection loss · human joint reverse constraint 

# 1 Introduction 

Recovering 3D human poses from a single image is an important foundation for machine perception and human activities understanding, with numerous applica-tions in action recognition, action prediction, and human-computer interaction.

In recent years, methods that use 3D annotation information to train deep neural networks have achieved excellent results, but the generalization of such meth-ods is limited by the costly 3D annotations. Thus many self-supervised meth-ods [3, 23, 35, 39] have been proposed to accomplish 3D human pose estimation using only easily available 2D annotations or images. Ensuring the 2D projection of the predicted 3D pose consistent with the original 2D input, i.e., reprojection consistency, is the theoretical basis of self-supervised human pose estimation. However, due to the depth ambiguity prob-lem of single view images, 3D human pose estimation cannot be accurately per-formed by reprojection consistency loss of single view images alone. Therefore, much effort has been made to solve this problem by introducing additional su-pervision information, such as using bone lengths [6, 13, 43], discriminators to verify the reasonableness of 2D/3D poses [3,32,41], or camera calibration [8,20]. Further, CanonPose [34] eliminates the reliance on additional information by introducing multi-view information in the training phase, extending the single-view reprojection consistency to multi-view cross-reprojection consistency. It achieves good accuracy when using only 2D supervision by decoupling pose and viewpoint. Therefore, many subsequent self-supervision methods [10, 16, 25, 31] are implemented based on this framework. The above methods based on the reprojection consistency loss have achieved promising results. However, there is very little work to pay attention to solving the inherent limitations of reprojection consistency: (1) Mismatched super-vision problem : Due to the lack of camera parameters and absolute position of the pose, self-supervised methods typically project the predicted 3D pose to the 2D plane via the orthographic projection model and compute the loss of consistency with the input 2D pose. In contrast, the input 2D pose, which acts Rotated Orthographic Projection for Self-Supervised 3D HPE 3

as a supervision, is processed by the perspective projection model. Inconsistent projection models lead to an offset between the supervision space and the pre-diction space, as shown in Fig.1(a), which limits the potential upper bound of model performance. (2) Z-axis overall reverse problem : The reprojection consistency loss lacks the constraint for pose absolute orientation in 3D space. Specifically when the Z-axis of the 3D pose is predicted to be reversed, the model can still make the 2D projection of the reversed pose consistent with the input pose by fitting out the wrong rotations, which severely harms the model stability, as shown in Fig.1(b). To tackle the mismatched supervision problem, we propose a rotated or-thographic projection(ROP) model, which achieves a geometric approximation of the perspective projection by adding a rotation operation calculated by the position of the reference point. Building upon this, we refine the selection of the reference point by considering human body structure, leading to the group rotated orthographic projection (G-ROP). By grouping the human body into distinct parts based on the relative distances of the joints, we calculate indi-vidual reference points and their corresponding rotations, further narrowing the gap with perspective projection. For the Z-axis overall reverse problem, we pro-pose joint reverse constraint, i.e., restrict the range of angles between the local reference plane and the end joints of the human body to penalize unrealistic poses in 3D space so that the model no longer confuses the Z-axis orientation. In this way, the skeletal kinematics prior can be used to effectively enhance the supervision of human poses in 3D space and supplement the shortcomings of 2D supervision. In summary, our main contributions are as follows: 

– We propose rotated orthographic projection and its enhancement, group rotated orthographic projection, making the first attempt to approximate perspective projection in a geometric way, which effectively solves the mis-matched supervision problem of reprojection consistency loss under the self-supervision paradigm. 

– We design a simple joint reverse loss based on the human structure prior, which effectively solves the Z-axis overall reverse problem and enhances the robustness of the model. 

– Extensive experiments demonstrate that our method, as a self-supervised framework for 3D human pose estimation, significantly outperforms state-of-the-art models on both Human3.6M and MPII-INF-3DHP datasets. 

# 2 Related Work 

2.1 3D Human Pose Estimation without 3D Ground Truth 

In the absence of 3D ground truth, many methods use multi-view images or generate 2D poses in new views to accomplish self-supervised 3D human pose estimation. Chen et al. [3] propose a cyclic consistency loss, generating new-view 2D poses by randomly projecting the predicted 3D poses and feeding them into 4 Yao et al. 

a lifting network. Wandt et al. [32] generate a more reasonable 2D pose by esti-mating the distribution of elevation angles. Although the above methods require only single view data for training, they have a dependency on the discriminator. Kocabas et al. [20] generate pseudo 3D ground truth by triangulation from multi-view data. Bouazizi et al. [2] add geometric consistency loss in 3D space and 2D reprojection loss to [20]. These methods of generating pseudo-labels require cam-era calibration. Iqbal et al. [13] use a multi-view consistency loss which forces the 3D poses predicted from different views to be consistent after rigid transforma-tion. However, bone length information is required to achieve this, otherwise a degenerate solution will result. Wandt et al. [34] propose a method called Canon-Pose that uses multi-view cross-reprojection loss by projecting the predicted 3D pose under a canonical coordinate system back to all camera views without any other information. They argued that multi-view reprojection is more effective compared to multi-view geometric consistency in 3D space. Our approach also follows this framework. 

2.2 3D-2D Projection 

One important part of reprojection loss is the 3D-2D projection. Since the ab-solute position of the 3D pose cannot be predicted from a single view, some methods [8, 15–17, 27, 34] use orthographic projection model to approximate the perspective projection model. However, the approximation produces significant errors when the person is close to the camera or the person is far from the optical axis. Some fully supervised methods that use reprojection as additional supervision try to use perspective projection by learning camera parameters. For instance, CLIFF [24] predicts the translation parameters of the camera from the images, while SPEC [19] additionally predicts the rotation parameters. However, these methods rely on geometric information in the image, resulting in poorly ro-bust in low texture contexts. Moreover, camera parameters cannot be estimated when relying only on 2D skeleton information. Among the self-supervised meth-ods, some of them [3, 41] use perspective projection after assuming the distance between the human and camera to be a fixed value, and such an assumption can equally result in severe mismatched supervision. Compared to these meth-ods, our approach increases the perspective effect by rotation while still using a simplified projection model. 

2.3 Human Prior Knowledge Constraints 

The structure of the human body contains a lot of prior information that can be utilized, e.g. range of motion [4,7,11] , skeletal symmetry [6,37]. Akhter et al. [1] propose joint angle constraints for moving bones in a Cartesian coordinate sys-tem by statistic of data, and some methods encode the feasibility of body poses using adversarial frameworks [14, 38] or normalizing flow [36, 42]. The methods mentioned above utilize statistical or learning approaches to implicitly learn the angular constraints of each joint, making the rationality of the constraints de-pendent on the quantity and diversity of the data. # 3 Method 

In this section, we first briefly analyze the discrepancies between orthographic and perspective projection models, then introduce the framework of our method, followed by presenting our Rotated Orthographic Projection(ROP), Group Ro-tated Orthographic Projection(G-ROP) and joint reverse loss.

Existing methods employ orthographic projection as an approximation, ig-noring the error ∆x o based on the strong assumption that there is a large dis-tance between the object and the camera. However, this approximation is no longer valid when the object is close to the camera or far away from the optical axis. When the object is translated, the orthographic projection is translation invariant while the perspec-tive projection shows a rotation-like perspective effect. Previous self-supervised methods have ignored the mismatched supervision problem due to the different projection methods, thus the network incorporates this bias into the predicted pose, specifically reflected as a slight rotation. This is the main reason why the accuracy of most methods improves dramatically after Procrustes alignment of the predicted pose to the ground truth.

3.4 Group Rotated Orthographic Projection 

According to Section 3.3, the rotation compensation matrix is related to the reference point, and a reasonable reference point setup yields a better geometric approximation of the perspective projection. A natural way is to set the average point of the 2D pose as the reference point. However, in some cases, the end joints are far from the center of the body, which can result in excessive or insufficient rotational compensation. As shown in Fig. 5, The red and orange points in the image indicate the reference points set according to the whole body and the right lower limb, respectively. According to the example on the left, the rotation compensation matrix calculated based on the red reference point has a larger angle around the x-axis, but the rotation compensation required for the right lower limb is smaller than this angle, which results in an error in the leg. 3.5 Joint Reverse Constraint 

The reprojection consistency loss lacks the constraint for absolute orientation in 3D space. When PoseNet outputs an illegal pose with an overall Z-axis reverse, CameraNet can still output a corresponding rotation to make the 2D projection of the wrong pose consistent with the input pose. To solve this problem, we propose joint reverse loss. In detail, we restrict the range of angles between the local reference plane and the end joints of the human body to penalize unreasonable poses in 3D space so that the model no longer confuses the Z-axis orientation. The main motion freedom of the elbow/knee joint is in extension and flexion, thus we can form a reference plane with elbow-shoulders/knee-hips, limiting the motion range of the end joints (wrist/ankle). For example, the right leg can only perform backward motion relative to the local plane consisting of the two hips and the right knee. The detailed imple-mentation is shown in Fig. 6, the red vector indicates the illegal joint structure, which has an angle in the range of [ 0◦,90 ◦] with the local reference plane normal vector. # 4 Experiment 

Datasets. Human3.6M [12] is the largest benchmark dataset. This dataset pro-vides information under four views, including image, 2D annotation, 3D annota-tion, and camera annotation. We follow the standard protocol using S1, S5, S6, S7, S8 for training and testing on S9 and S10. To achieve complete inference from images to 3D poses, we also use the 2D poses obtained from CPN [5] provided by [28]. MPI-INF-3DHP [26] is another commonly used large-scale dataset that offers more difficult poses and perspectives than Human3.6M. Following [30], we chose eight of the fourteen views provided, excluding large-angle overlooks. We also follow the officially provided training and test set settings, where three scenes are included in the test set: green screen, non-green screen and outdoor. 

Metrics. For the evaluation of Human3.6M and MPI-INF-3DHP, we calcu-late the mean-per-joint-position-error (MPJPE), which quantifies the mean Eu-clidean distance between predicted and actual joint coordinates. Due to the ab-sence of metric data in a self-supervised setting, we scale-normalize the predicted 3D poses to match the ground truth, i.e., N-MPJPE [29]. Another common pro-tocol first performs the Procrustes alignment (including scaling and rotating) between the predicted pose and ground truth before calculating MPJPE, also known as P-MPJPE. For MPI-INF-3DHP, we also used the Percentage of Correct Keypoints (PCK) for validation. It is the percentage of predicted joints that are within a distance of 150mm or lower to their corresponding ground truth joint. 

Implementation details. Our network framework follows CanonPose, using two branches to predict pose and coordinate system rotation respectively, but with changes made for each network structure: (1) Replacing PoseNet from MLP to SemGCN [44], and CameraNet retains the MLP structure, which is because we consider Graph Convolutional Network to be more suitable for skeleton in-put (2) For the output of CameraNet, replacing axis-angle representations with 6D continuous rotation representations [45]. This modification addresses the dis-continuity inherent in axis-angle representations, which renders the network less robust, particularly when output angles approach 0◦or 180 ◦. # 5 Conclusion 

In this work, we propose rotated orthographic projection, which achieves a ge-ometric approximation of the perspective projection by adding a rotation op-eration before the orthographic projection. It effectively solves the mismatched supervision problem of reprojection consistency loss under the self-supervision paradigm. Additionally, we optimize the reference point selection according to the human body structure and propose group rotated orthographic projection, which further narrows the gap with the perspective projection model. Mean-while, we design a simple joint reverse loss based on the human structure prior, which effectively solves the Z-axis overall reverse problem and enhances the ro-bustness of the model. Our improvement greatly enhances the performance of the framework for self-supervised 3D human pose estimation using multi-view cross-reprojection. Extensive experiments demonstrate that our method significantly outperforms state-of-the-art models on both Human3.6M and MPII-INF-3DHP datasets.
