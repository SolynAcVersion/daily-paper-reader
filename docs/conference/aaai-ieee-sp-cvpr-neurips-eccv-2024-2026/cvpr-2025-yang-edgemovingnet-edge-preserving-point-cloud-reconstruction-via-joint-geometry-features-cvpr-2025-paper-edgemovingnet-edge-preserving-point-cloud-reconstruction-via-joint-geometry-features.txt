# Abstract 

Point cloud reconstruction is a critical process in 3D repre-sentation and reverse engineering. When it comes to CAD models, edges are significant features that play a crucial role in characterizing the geometry of 3D shapes. How-ever, few points are exactly sampled on edges during acqui-sition, resulting in apparent artifacts for the reconstruction task. Upsampling point cloud is a direct technical route, but there is a main challenge that the upsampled points may not align with the model edge accurately. To over-come this, we develop an integrated framework to estimate edges by joint regression of three geometry features—point-to-edge direction, point-to-edge distance and point normal. Benefiting these features, we implement a novel refinement process to move and produce more points which lie ac-curately on edges of the model, allowing for high-quality edge-preserving reconstruction. Experiments and compar-isons against previous methods demonstrate our method’s effectiveness and superiority. 

# 1. Introduction 

Reconstructing mesh from raw point clouds is a fundamen-tal task in reverse engineering and computer-aided design. Over the years, a wide range of techniques have been de-veloped to tackle this problem. As for high quality recon-struction, edge features serve as critical geometric cues in characterizing the structure of objects, especially for CAD models. Traditional reconstruction methods such as Poisson [15] and Voronoi-based [5] method fail to preserve sharp edges well (See Fig. 1). In this paper, we take point clouds without normal as input and adopt a two-stage method to achieve edge-preserving surface reconstruction results. The challenges come from two aspects. On the one hand, the general input point cloud may be sparse and contain very few points accurately located on the edges of the base geometry. While certain point consolidation [12, 13, 25, 26]

Additionally, some approaches [21, 24, 31, 41] involve partitioning the given points into patches and fitting a smooth surface to each patch. However, identifying the optimal decomposition that reveals the structure of the underlying model remains chal-lenging. On the other hand, CAD models exhibit a wide va-riety of geometric shapes. Algorithms [4, 19, 33, 47] relying on analytic models of geometric features (such as curvature and its derivatives) often necessitate manual parameter tun-ing per object, rendering them less suitable for large collec-tions of data. In contrast, data-driven, learning based meth-ods [6, 14, 36, 43] offer a natural alternative. They leverage global information from training datasets and autonomously adapt to specific input shapes without user intervention. Our paper introduces EdgeMovingNet and a refinement process to address the aforementioned challenges. In the first stage, our focus is on developing a robust fea-ture extraction algorithm for the sampled point cloud. Some characteristics of the point cloud include: (1) sparse distri-bution, with points rarely aligning directly with (edge) fea-tures; (2) noisy distribution; (3) absence of normals. We in-troduce EdgeMovingNet—a deep-learning-based approach that estimates edges by joint prediction of sharp geomet-ric features (point-to-edge direction and point-to-edge dis-tance) as well as normal information, in order to handle this type of data. The advantage of employing continuous feature func-tions—compared to binary classification (feature vs. non-feature points)—is its robustness for samples that are mis-aligned with features or noisy. Given the sparsity of sam-ples, we adopt a global prediction strategy for the entire shape rather than handling points in a patch-based manner. In the second stage, we introduce a refinement process to achieve high-quality surface reconstruction using the fea-tures obtained from EdgeMovingNet. We initiate the pro-cess by generating and filtering additional points located on potential geometry edges. Subsequently, we leverage the predicted basic normal information to optimize the posi-tions of these edge points. After that, we apply a basic re-construction solver (like Poisson reconstruction [15]) on the initial point cloud, resulting in a base surface. In the final step, we adjust the surface connections based on the opti-mized edge point locations, achieving an edge-preserving reconstruction. We demonstrate that our method performs favorably to the classical and learning-based state-of-the-art methods. In summary, our contributions include: • We propose a new learning-based framework called EdgeMovingNet, specifically designed to extract edges from point clouds by jointly estimation of point-to-edge direction, point-to-edge distance, and point normal. • We design a refinement process to produce additional points that align precisely with edges of geometry and utilize a relocation strategy to reconstruct an edge-preserving mesh by these edge points. • We show our good qualitative and quantitative results on point cloud reconstruction benchmarks, demonstrating the effectiveness of the proposed method. 

# 2. Related work 

Point cloud consolidation. Resampling, downsampling, and upsampling are commonly employed techniques to gen-erate feature consolidated point clouds for various down-stream applications. In the past few decades, a large amount of approaches concerning point cloud consolida-tion [2, 3, 12, 13, 25, 26] has been developed. Although the point density near sharp features is increased, the con-solidated points are not located on edges precisely. In recent years, the advancement of deep learning has revealed the potential of neural networks in handling unordered point cloud data. PointNet [35], PointNet++ [36], PointCNN [23], DGCNN [43], Point Transformer [11, 52], and PointMLP [30] are popular backbones for point cloud learning. Numerous learning-based methods for point cloud upsampling [10, 22, 37, 38, 48, 49, 51] and downsampling [9, 20] have been proposed. Yu et al . [50] presented EC-Net, the first learnable edge-aware method to upsample points consolidated near edges by using a point-to-edge loss to train the network. Metzer et al. [32] introduced a self-sampling network for neural point cloud consolidation which learns only from the input point cloud. However, their ability to capture edges with high precision is limited. Wang et al. [42] proposed the learning-based PIE-Net to classify edge points, corner points and non-edge points from point clouds, then rep-resent edges as a collection of parametric curves (lines, circles and B-spline curves). Liu et al. [27] introduced an end-to-end trainable deep network that detects corners and connections, enabling direct conversion of a point cloud into a linear wireframe model. Matveev et al. [31] presented DEF, a method that predicts the distance to edges of each point for effective edge reconstruction. However, the performance of these methods is limited when the sampled point cloud is not dense enough. 

Feature preserving surface reconstruction. Reconstruct-ing surface from point clouds to ultimately form a mesh has been a longstanding problem in computer graphics. Tra-ditional methods (such as Poisson reconstruction [15, 16]) rely on point normals as additional input and have difficulty in preserving sharp features effectively, which is of great significance in the field of CAD. Salman et al. [40] pro-posed a feature detection process based on the covariance matrices of Voronoi cells to extract a set of sharp features, facilitating feature-preserving mesh generation. Dey et al. [8] suggested identifying and reconstructing feature curves by combining the Gaussian-weighted graph Laplacian and the Reeb graphs. Xiong et al. [46] presented a framework that optimizes mesh geometry and connectivity simultane-ously from the unoriented point cloud based on dictionary learning. Boulch and Marlet [6] proposed POCO, an im-plicit neural network through point cloud convolutions to answer occupancy queries. Ma et al. [29] introduced pre-dictive context priors by learning predictive queries for each specific point cloud, getting a global signed distance func-tions and using marching cubes [28] to reconstruct surfaces. Ren et al. [39] purposes GeoUDF which is a geometry-guided learning method for UDF and its gradient estimation for surface reconstruction. Huang et al. [14] presents Neu-ral Kernel Surface Reconstruction (NKSR) upon the Neu-ral Kernel Fields (NKF) [44] representation. Xu et al. [47] presented RFEPS, a multistage optimization algorithm to generate polygonal surfaces interpolated by the augmented point, but it requires normal as input. Empirical evidence indicates that achieving feature-preserving reconstruction is challenging when dealing with sparse input points or lack-ing reliable normals.

In this section, we provide a detailed description of our method. The whole pipeline is summarized in Fig. 2. 22151 Figure 2. Given a raw point cloud, our EdgeMovingNet predicts three geometry features from it: point normal, point-to-edge direction and point-to-edge distance. Combining these features, our method produces additional edge points lying precisely on sharp features and finally reconstructs an edge-preserving mesh. 

In Sec. 3.1, we introduce our feature extraction approach by training EdgeMovingNet to predict point-wise normal, point-to-edge direction and point-to-edge distance jointly. ## 3.1. Feature extraction 

For a point cloud sampled without normal, we initially nor-malize its center to origin and scale it within a unit sphere. Next we define three crucial point-wise geometric features on it: point normal n, point-to-edge direction ne and point-to-edge distance de. As illustrated in Fig. 3a, point-to-edge direction ne is defined by the direction (unit vector) to the closest point on the nearest sharp edge, and point-to-edge distance de is defined by the distance between them. With these features in place, we can project a point c onto its near-est sharp edge using the formula: ce = c + ne ∗ de. Here ce

represents the projected edge point. All features are extracted in a learning-based way. We present EdgeMovingNet on the core idea of jointly learn-ing and predicting these three features. Since the point cloud may be sparse and consistent normal orientation re-quires entire sharp prior, EdgeMovingNet takes whole sam-pled point cloud as input instead of dividing it into smaller patches. ## 3.4. Edge-preserving reconstruction 

The last step in the process involves surface reconstruction. Based on the final edge points denoted as Ce′, we leverage a relocation strategy to achieve edge-preserving reconstruc-tion. We first apply Poisson [15] reconstruction solver to the original input point cloud with our predicted normals to generate a base surface (see Fig. 7a) (This step can be substituted with other reconstruction solvers, such as [5]). Then, we move the vertices in reconstructed mesh to their nearest edge points in Ce′ within the threshold δ′ mentioned in Sec. 3.3. This relocation does not affect vertex connec-tion but rather adjusts their positions to cover edges. By implementing this strategy, an edge-preserving reconstruc-tion result can be achieved (see Fig. 7b).

# 4. Experiments 

## 4.1. Implementation details 

We build our dataset on a portion of ABC dataset [18], a large source of 3D CAD models with labeled edges. We select 13,369 objects with sharp edges from ABC and divide them into three parts: 10,000 models for training, 2,000 for validation and 1,369 for testing. We sample 10k points from each model and compute their ground-truth point-to-edge directions and distances before training. We implement our model in PyTorch [34]. We use Adam [17] optimizer with β = (0.9, 0.999) and weight decay 0.002 for a learning rate 1e-4. Our EdgeMovingNet is trained with batch size 8 for 200 epochs which takes 3 days on two NVIDIA-2080Ti GPUs. We use the LBFGS [53] func-tion for solving constrained optimization problems defined in Sec. 3.3. 

## 4.2. Comparisons 

To demonstrate the effectiveness of our method, we com-pare it against classical and state-of-the-art methods on point cloud reconstruction or edge consolidation, including Poisson [15], Voronoi [5], POCO [6], PCP [29], RFEPS [47], GeoUDF [39] and NKSR [14]. We use 8,192 points sampled from ABC [18] dataset as input for all methods ex-cept POCO [6], which requires 10,000 points according to its pre-trained models. Poisson [15] and RFEPS [47] re-quire point normal as additional input, so we offer ground-truth normals for them. For other methods including ours, the input is only 3D coordinates. 

Quantitative comparison. To evaluate the accuracy of the reconstructed mesh, we use three metrics: Chamfer dis-tance (CD), Hausdorff dance (HD), and normal consistency (NC) to measure the approximation error between the re-sults and the ground truth model. Meanwhile, we assess precision, recall, and F-score by sampling points from the reconstructed mesh. Points are considered matched if there is at least one ground truth point with an L2 distance smaller than 0.01. Statistics of those measurements for different methods are presented in Tab. 1. Our method, without any additional input, achieves the best results in metrics of Chamfer distance, Hausdorff distance, precision, recall and F-score, and achieves competitive result in evaluation of normal consistency.

## 4.5. Limitations 

In cases where the model has too thin structures or intricate details, such as keyboard, our method may not accurately estimate edge points due to EdgeMovingNet’s non-patch-based nature. And the performance of our method will drop if the input point cloud contains too much noise or outliers, necessitating a pre-denoising step. 

# 5. Conclusion 

In this paper, we present a novel framework to reconstruct high-quality meshes from sparse point cloud data. The main concept involves estimating additional edge points to guide edge-preserving reconstruction. We present Edge-MovingNet, a feature extraction network that jointly esti-mates point-to-edge direction, point-to-edge distance and point normal. A refinement process and a relocating strat-egy is adopted for the final edge-preserving reconstruction. Extensive experimental results show that our method out-performs previous methods both quantitatively and qualita-tively, demonstrating its effectiveness and usefulness. 22157 Acknowledgments
